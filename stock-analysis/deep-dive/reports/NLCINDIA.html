NLC India Limited

NSE: NLCINDIA · BSE: 513683 · FY2022–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing and investor-presentation synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: A 70-year-old, government-controlled lignite-and-coal miner turned integrated power generator just delivered its best-ever year — FY26 consolidated revenue ₹17,490 Cr (+14%), PAT ₹3,769 Cr (+39%), capex ₹9,131 Cr (+18%, an all-time high) — while its FY26 statutory auditors attached a material-uncertainty-relating-to-going-concern paragraph over land shortage at its flagship Neyveli lignite mines, its Q1 FY27 CMD and Director (Power) posts are both being run on temporary “additional charge” by Ministry of Coal bureaucrats after back-to-back retirements, its board ran for over a year without a single independent director in apparent breach of SEBI's minimum-independence rule, and it fully wrote off ₹1,454 Cr of advances to a defaulting EPC contractor on its flagship UP thermal project. Free cash flow was FY26's first negative year in this dataset (−₹2,779 Cr) purely because capex outran even a record operating cash flow, funding a genuinely enormous ₹1.01 lakh crore capacity build-out through 2030. The government trimmed its stake by 2.73% in an August 2026 OFS even as Tamil Nadu's Chief Minister publicly objected to further disinvestment.

01

What NLC India does, and how it has grown

India's dominant lignite miner and a fast-diversifying integrated power generator, still 72% owned by the Government of India, now mid-way through a ₹1.01 lakh crore capacity build-out to 2030.

Incorporated in 1956 as Neyveli Lignite Corporation and renamed NLC India Limited in April 2016, the company is a Navratna central public-sector enterprise headquartered in Neyveli, Tamil Nadu, operating under the administrative control of the Ministry of Coal. Its business spans three reported segments — Mining (lignite and coal), Thermal Power (captive/standalone plus JV capacity via subsidiaries NTPL and NUPPL), and Renewable Power (solar, wind and increasingly battery storage, run mostly through subsidiary NLC India Renewables Ltd, "NIRL") — plus a smaller mining/solar consultancy line and an emerging critical-minerals push. As of the FY26 investor presentation the company operates 6 mines and 17 power plants, with total mining capacity of 50.10 MTPA (30 MTPA lignite + 20 MTPA coal), thermal capacity of 5,960 MW, and renewable capacity of 1,734 MW (total installed capacity ≈7,694 MW/50 MTPA equivalent). Management's own claim, unverified independently in this dataset but consistent with segment mining-output data below: NLC India contributes approximately 51% of India's national lignite output, with Tamil Nadu (where its flagship Neyveli mines sit) holding roughly 79% of the country's lignite resource base — a genuine, geology-anchored moat that is also the source of its single biggest disclosed risk (§05).

The FY24 revenue dip (−19.6%) was a high base-effect correction after an exceptional FY23 (fuel/power price spike year across Indian utilities), not a demand problem — FY24 EBITDA and PAT both grew regardless. Since then growth has been genuinely broad-based: FY25 and FY26 both delivered double-digit revenue growth alongside margin expansion, capacity additions, and management's own repeated "all-time-highest-ever" framing for revenue, PAT, EBITDA, capex and net worth in FY26 specifically (13-May-2026 press release). RONW nearly tripled from FY23 (9.4%) to FY26 (17.5%) on genuine margin and earnings-power expansion rather than financial leverage — consolidated debt/equity actually fell over the same window (1.47x→1.29x, §05).

Capacity mix: from a pure thermal generator to a diversified energy platform

The company's own strategic narrative frames a structural pivot: thermal capacity was 100% of the mix at incorporation (1970s), still 87% by FY2017, and is down to 77% today (FY26: 5,960 MW thermal vs 1,734 MW renewable). Management's own FY2030 target flips this further — renewable capacity is guided to reach 10,110 MW (5.5x current) against 10,020 MW thermal, taking the group to a roughly 50/50 thermal/renewable mix by 2030, with an additional 104.35 MTPA mining capacity (from 50.10 MTPA today) and a new diversification bucket (silica sand from mine overburden, coal/lignite gasification, critical minerals). See §03 and §05 for the detailed project pipeline and capex allocation behind this target, and §10 for whether the market is currently paying for it.

The moat, and its limits

NLC India's moat is largely a captive, mine-mouth cost advantage: it owns the lignite/coal that fuels its own thermal plants, minimizing logistics cost versus fuel-imported peers, and sells power under long-tenure, cost-plus-regulated PPAs to state DISCOMs (governed by CERC tariff regulations, §04) rather than competing on open-market pricing. This is a genuine structural advantage but not an unlimited one: it depends on continued access to mineable land at Neyveli, which the company's own FY26 statutory auditors flagged as a going-concern-level uncertainty (§05) — the moat and the single biggest disclosed risk in this report trace back to the same asset. Diversification into renewables and thermal JVs outside Tamil Nadu (Ghatampur in Uttar Pradesh, planned Odisha and Rajasthan projects) is explicitly framed by management as reducing this single-geography, single-fuel dependency over the coming decade.

What actually moves the quarterly number: regulatory deferral accounting

Under CERC's cost-plus tariff framework, NLC India recognizes a "Net Movement in Regulatory Deferral Account Balances" line between operating profit and PBT — essentially income/expense truing-up against interim vs. final tariff orders. ⚠
TPS-II under-recovery: a recurring cost drag that has itself missed its own guidanceManagement referenced an internal target of keeping TPS-II's tariff under-recovery below roughly ₹300 Cr for FY26 on an earlier call; by nine months into FY26 the actual cumulative under-recovery had already reached ₹623 Cr — more than double the full-year target with one quarter still to run. Q1 FY27's 48% PAT decline: a tax base-effect, not an operating deterioration

Q1 FY27 consolidated PBT grew 10% YoY (₹651.6 Cr vs ₹593.6 Cr) and operating performance held broadly steady, yet PAT fell 48% because the prior-year quarter (Q1 FY26) booked a net tax benefit of −₹245.6 Cr (a large deferred-tax credit), while Q1 FY27 booked a normal tax expense of ₹215.3 Cr — a roughly ₹461 Cr negative swing in the tax line alone, more than explaining the entire PAT decline. This is exactly the kind of divergence the underlying tabulation is built to catch: taken at face value, the "PAT nearly halved" headline that several news aggregators ran with (§02) materially understates the quarter's actual operating performance.

Forward margin guidance

The company's own FY2030 roadmap (§10) guides EBITDA margin (company definition, on total income) from 42.7% in FY26 to 50.6% by FY30, alongside revenue more than doubling to ₹37,713 Cr. Note this uses a different EBITDA definition/base than the "operating margin" (22.39% FY26) figure used elsewhere in this report — the two are not directly comparable and are presented separately rather than blended, consistent with this report's sourcing discipline.

How it's being funded: retained capital, a new OFS, and a widening debt programme

The August-2026 Offer for Sale (2.73% of GoI's stake, oversubscribed on the institutional tranche, raising ₹1,260–1,760 Cr — source language differs marginally between the standalone and consolidated filing notes and is presented as a range rather than resolved to false precision) is a stake sale by the government, not a primary capital raise for NLCIL itself, so it does not directly fund the capex programme; NLCIL's own funding mix instead leans on retained earnings (net worth +15% YoY in FY26), an expanding rated-debt programme (India Ratings assigned a fresh IND AAA/Stable rating on ₹1,102 Cr of new bank facilities in Aug-2026, on top of affirmed AAA/A1+ ratings across ₹2,500 Cr of NCDs and ₹6,000 Cr of commercial paper), and external commercial borrowing (a EUR 100 million KfW, Germany facility tied up during FY26). Consolidated debt/equity has actually declined over five years (1.57x→1.29x FY22-FY26) even as the absolute capex base roughly quadrupled — the leverage math still works today, but the FY2030 plan implies a further, much larger step-up in both debt and equity capital that the current AAA rating trajectory will need to keep pace with.

Related-party and subsidiary structure

The consolidated group runs through five direct subsidiaries (NTPL 89%, NUPPL 51%, NIRL 100%, NIGEL 100%, NRPL 74%), three step-down NIRL subsidiaries, one associate (MNH Shakti) and one joint venture (Coal Lignite Urja Vikas), plus the newly-signed 50:50 NALCO JV. Related-party transactions disclosed at the annual-report level were modest relative to group revenue in both FY24 and FY25 (no material scale flag found); the more notable structural item is that the Comptroller & Auditor General's FY26 supplementary audit issued nil/clean comments for every consolidated entity except NIGEL (NLC India Green Energy Ltd), which received only a "non-audit certificate" — i.e., CAG did not complete its usual supplementary audit procedure for the group's dedicated green-energy diversification subsidiary for FY26, a minor but notable gap in an otherwise clean audit sweep.

Management, board & governance

A textbook PSU governance profile — Government of India control, Ministry of Coal-appointed leadership, CAG audit oversight — currently running with both its top executive posts on temporary "additional charge" and a multi-year pattern of falling short of SEBI's independent-director requirement.

A company mid-way through its largest-ever capex programme and a planned subsidiary IPO is, as of the most recent filings reviewed, being run without a permanent CEO-equivalent or a permanent power-business head.

Board composition appears to have run without a single independent director for an extended periodThe May-2026 investor presentation's own "Composition of Board of Directors" slide lists only the CMD, two part-time official (government nominee) directors, and five functional/whole-time directors — zero independent directors. The government-control picture, and what disinvestment means here

Government of India holds 72.20% as of 15 May 2026 (pre-OFS), reduced to an implied ~69.5% after the 2.73% OFS in August 2026 (§05) — majority government control is not in question either before or after. As a Navratna CPSE, NLC India gets meaningfully greater operational and capex-approval autonomy than a standard PSU, but remains subject to CAG supplementary audit (clean for FY26, §05), Ministry of Coal control over top appointments (as evidenced directly by the CMD/Director changes above), DIPAM oversight of dividend policy (an annual-report-era filing referenced the company needing to seek an exemption from DIPAM's minimum-dividend guideline in at least one prior year), and periodic disinvestment at the government's discretion, which the Tamil Nadu state government has publicly, if so far unsuccessfully, opposed (§02).

Auditors & other findings

  • Statutory auditors: Joint auditors Sundaram & Srinivasan and PKF Sridhar & Santhanam LLP issued unmodified/clean opinions on both standalone and consolidated FY26 results, subject to the going-concern disclosure paragraph and the VSVS Emphasis of Matter (§05, both explicitly non-qualifying). The joint-auditor pairing has rotated at least once across this dataset (a predecessor, Chaturvedi & Co LLP, is referenced as having audited the FY25 comparative figures) — consistent with mandated CAG/statutory audit rotation for PSUs, not itself a red flag.
  • Contingent liabilities: The FY24 Annual Report discloses total contingent liabilities of approximately ₹34,370 Cr (standalone) — dominated by land-acquisition compensation claims (~₹7,329 Cr) and tax demands (~₹1,420 Cr), with the balance largely bank guarantees and other claims. This is roughly 3.3x FY24 standalone revenue; while contingent liabilities of this scale are not unusual for a capital-intensive, land-dependent PSU miner with a multi-decade operating history, the land-compensation component is directly relevant context for the going-concern flag in §05.
  • Cybersecurity/compliance lapse: A malware incident affecting NLCIL servers on 2 July 2024 was reported to SEBI four days later (6 July 2024) rather than within the regulator's prescribed disclosure window — a minor but real compliance lapse on cybersecurity incident reporting, noted in the FY24-era filings reviewed.
  • CSR: FY24 CSR spend of ₹47.36 Cr against a statutory obligation of ₹40.27 Cr — compliant, and modestly above the mandated minimum.

Other disclosure insights worth watching

Structural moves that could move the stock more than any single quarter's P&L — a subsidiary IPO, a new captive-JV model, and a critical-minerals option few investors are pricing in yet.

NLC India Renewables Ltd (NIRL): the value-unlock most likely to matter next

Seven operational renewable assets totalling 1.4 GW were transferred from NLCIL to NIRL during FY26 as part of an explicit asset-monetisation exercise, and GoI has approved an "Alternative Mechanism" for listing NIRL via a fresh equity issue plus disinvestment of up to 25% of NLCIL's stake in NIRL through a public offering in the domestic market. Combined with CCEA's approval of a ₹7,000 Cr investment ceiling for NIRL beyond standard Navratna subsidiary limits, this reads as a genuine, GoI-sanctioned value-unlock event in preparation, guided (with modest slippage, §03) toward a listing around September 2026. NIRL's own order pipeline has been prolific in the news window alone: SECI wind (200 MW, Koppal), GUVNL solar (900 MW LoI), Gujarat BESS auction win (275 MW), plus multiple JVs (NCRTC, MAHAPREIT, and a proposed one with PTC India) — a subsidiary being built out fast in the run-up to a listing.

The NALCO captive-power JV: a template for future thermal growth

The 8-July-2026 joint venture with National Aluminium Company (50:50, 1,080 MW / 4x270 MW coal-based captive thermal plant at Angul, Odisha, backed by a 25-year Fuel Supply Agreement from NLCIL's own Machhakata coal mine and a 25-year Section-62-regulated PPA for 100% of NALCO's offtake) is a genuinely different growth model from NLCIL's traditional standalone-utility approach: a captive-plus-regulated-return structure anchored to a single large industrial customer's own expansion plans, using NLCIL's own coal. If replicated, this JV model could become a meaningful, lower-execution-risk growth lever alongside the DISCOM-facing thermal and merchant/competitive-bid renewable pipelines.

Critical minerals and coal-by-product diversification: optionality, not yet revenue

NLCIL has secured composite exploration licences for phosphorite and limestone blocks in Chhattisgarh (Semhardih and Raipura), and commissioned a 1.0 MTPA washed-silica-sand plant from Mine-I overburden in February 2026 with commercial sales begun (TAMIN floated a tender for Mine-IA M-Sand in May 2026). The ₹10,669 Cr "Diversification" bucket of the FY2030 capex plan (§05) explicitly includes coal/lignite gasification. None of this shows up as a material revenue line yet, but it is a genuine option on future demand for critical minerals and by-product monetisation that a market pricing NLC India purely as a lignite-and-thermal utility may be under-crediting.

All-time-high share price, then a ~30% pullback

NLC India traded at an all-time-high ₹387.80 (14 May 2026, NSE) with market capitalisation briefly exceeding ₹48,900 Cr (per the company's own 15-May-2026 investor presentation) — coinciding almost exactly with the FY26 results release and the going-concern disclosure becoming public. The stock has since fallen to ₹269.80 (22 Aug 2026), a decline of roughly 30% from that peak over just over three months, against a 52-week range of ₹222.0–₹387.8. Whether this reflects the market digesting the going-concern/governance flags, broader utility-sector rotation, profit-taking after the OFS-driven supply, or some combination is not resolvable from this dataset alone, but the magnitude and timing are notable enough to flag explicitly (§10).

Bottom line

What's good: genuinely broad-based five-year financial improvement — revenue nearly doubled, PAT more than tripled, RONW roughly doubled, and debt/equity actually declined even as capex quadrupled (FY22–FY26); FY26 was management's own "all-time-highest-ever" year on revenue, PAT, EBITDA, capex and net worth simultaneously; a dominant, geology-anchored ~51% share of India's lignite output; a credible, capital-committed ₹1.01 lakh crore FY2030 roadmap with capex execution that has historically matched or beaten its own targets; unanimous AAA/Stable credit ratings across every rating agency reviewed, with a freshly assigned rating on new debt as recently as August 2026; strong, cash-backed earnings quality on every annual cross-check performed (CFO has never fallen short of PAT in five years); and a fast-moving, apparently well-executed renewables build-out (NIRL) heading toward a GoI-sanctioned partial listing.

What's not good: the FY26 statutory auditors attached a material-uncertainty-relating-to-going-concern paragraph over land availability at the company's core Neyveli lignite mines — a serious flag on the exact asset the company's moat depends on, and one that received essentially zero coverage in the news sources reviewed for this report; both the CMD and Director (Power) posts are, as of the most recent filings, held on temporary additional charge following back-to-back retirements, with no permanent successor confirmed in this dataset; the board appears to have run for an extended period with zero independent directors, a likely SEBI LODR compliance gap only partially remedied by a single July-2026 appointment; ₹1,454 Cr was fully written off against a defaulting EPC contractor on the flagship Ghatampur project; FY26 free cash flow turned negative for the first time in this five-year dataset as record capex outran even record operating cash flow; a persistent, four-year-old DISCOM billing dispute continues to grow via accrued interest; the current ratio has structurally deteriorated from 1.38x to 0.78–0.80x over three years; and Pachwara South coal production and Ghatampur's final unit both landed materially behind management's own earlier guidance.

How management has navigated it: operationally competently and financially conservatively — capex has consistently kept pace with or exceeded stated targets, credit ratings have never wavered from AAA, and the FY2030 roadmap is detailed and specific rather than vague. Governance continuity has been handled less smoothly: two senior retirements within a month of each other were bridged with interim appointments rather than pre-planned succession, and the independent-director shortfall was allowed to persist for a long stretch before a single appointment in mid-2026. Disclosure itself has been genuinely thorough at the primary-filing level (the going-concern paragraph, the BGR-ESL write-off, and the TPS-II under-recovery miss are all disclosed, not hidden) even though none of it surfaces in the secondary news coverage a casual investor would see.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood the next 12 months resolve constructively (i.e., broadly toward the base/bull scenarios above) from today's starting price: moderate, roughly 45–55%. The underlying operating and financial trend is genuinely strong and well-documented across five years of clean, cash-backed earnings growth, a real and growing lignite/thermal/renewable asset base, and a credibly-funded expansion plan — but this report's own tabulation surfaces a going-concern audit flag, a leadership vacuum at the top of the house, and a governance-compliance gap that collectively sit uncomfortably close together in time (all clustered in the May–July 2026 filing window) for a company simultaneously executing its largest-ever capex programme and preparing a subsidiary IPO. None of these three issues is necessarily fatal individually, and the valuation (11x trailing, 1.7x book) already prices in some caution — but a reader should not treat NLC India as a clean, low-risk PSU-utility compounder purely on the strength of its very good five-year P&L trend without weighing the §05/§07 flags explicitly.
