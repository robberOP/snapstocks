Equity Research Note · Independent Power Producer / Thermal & Hydro Generation

Jaiprakash Power Ventures Limited

NSE: JPPOWER · BSE: 532627 · FY2023–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing and annual-report synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: JPPOWER just changed hands. Its promoter, Jaiprakash Associates Limited (JAL), went through NCLT insolvency; Adani Enterprises won JAL's resolution plan in March 2026; and as part of implementing that plan, JAL's entire 24% stake in JPPOWER was sold to Adani Power for ₹2,993.6 crore on 20 May 2026, followed two days later by the wholesale resignation of every Jaypee-era executive director and two independent directors, replaced by three Adani Power nominees. That governance reset landed on top of a business whose FY26 numbers were already deteriorating: consolidated revenue grew a thin 1.5% while consolidated PAT fell 44.6% (₹813.6 Cr → ₹450.6 Cr) as plant-level operating profit (PBIDT) fell 24% even as plant load factors improved at both thermal stations — a margin story, not a volume story. Capex collapsed to just ₹7.9 crore against ₹472.8 crore of depreciation, the coal-mining subsidiary business is being surrendered, no dividend has been paid, and disclosed contingent claims against the company nearly quadrupled to ₹9,126 crore — a sum close to the entire market capitalisation — driven mainly by an Andhra Pradesh mining-department dispute inherited from a defunct sand-mining contract. Adani Power itself has already re-pledged 19% of the company's shares to its own acquisition lenders. This is a leveraged-buyout-style change of control landing on a still-distressed balance sheet, not a clean growth re-rating.

01

What JPPOWER does, and how it got here

A 2,220 MW thermal-and-hydro generator built by the Jaypee Group, rebuilt once already through a 2019 debt restructuring, now changing owners a second time via its parent's insolvency.

Incorporated in 1994 as Jaiprakash Hydro-Power Limited and renamed Jaiprakash Power Ventures Limited in December 2009, the New Delhi-headquartered company owns and operates three power plants totalling 2,220 MW: the 400 MW Vishnuprayag hydro-electric plant in Chamoli, Uttarakhand (operating since October 2006); the 500 MW (2×250 MW) Bina thermal plant in Sagar, Madhya Pradesh (since 2012–13); and the 1,320 MW (2×660 MW) Nigrie supercritical thermal plant in Singrauli, Madhya Pradesh (since 2014–15). It also runs a 2 MTPA cement grinding unit at Nigrie (currently generating nil turnover per the FY26 Annual Report) and, until recently, the captive Amelia (North) coal mine (2.8 MTPA operating capacity against a 3.92 MTPA licence) feeding the Nigrie plant, plus the right of exploration on the adjacent Bandha North coal block (MarketData/overview.md; AnnualReport FY25-26).

Revenue mix: a mix of regulated long-term PPAs and merchant exposure

Each plant sells power under a different structure. Vishnuprayag supplies 88% of net generation to Uttar Pradesh Power Corporation (UPPCL) under a long-term PPA, with the remaining 12% given free to the Uttarakhand government. Bina supplies 70% of installed capacity (65% to Madhya Pradesh Power Management Co. at MPERC-determined tariff, plus 5% to the state government) with the balance sold as merchant power on exchanges or bilaterally. Nigrie supplies roughly 37.5% long-term (30% + 7.5%) to the same Madhya Pradesh counterparties, with a larger merchant tranche (5,799 MU of the plant's 10,267.5 MU gross FY26 generation was sold as merchant sales). The FY26 Annual Report's MD&A is candid that this merchant tail is a real operating risk, not just an upside optionality: it states MPPMCL has been "giving restricted schedule to BINA TPP... erratic and fluctuating schedules of dispatch... forcing Company to sell balance power to power exchanges at un-remunerative tariff."

This is the central tension of the FY26 year, carried through this whole report: the plants themselves ran better — PLF at both thermal stations rose meaningfully, and total saleable generation grew 9.0% — yet, as §03–04 show, revenue barely moved and profit fell sharply. The gap sits in realisation (merchant/exchange tariffs, MPPMCL curtailment) and in costs below the operating line, not in the plants' physical performance.

A company that has already been restructured once

JPPOWER's balance sheet still carries the scars of a 2019 "Framework Agreement" debt restructuring with its lenders, including ₹3,805.53 crore of Compulsorily Convertible Cumulative Preference Shares (CCPS, 29-year maturity) issued to lenders as part of that plan — see §05 for why this remains a live equity-dilution overhang. Coal mining has now become a source of retreat rather than growth: in May 2026 the Board approved surrendering both the Amelia (North) and Bandha North coal mines, citing "recent changes in Government coal policies, their economic viability and sociological aspects" (BSE filing, 16 May 2026) — a formal admission that a core input-security asset is no longer viable to run.

The moat, such as it's evidenced

The real asset is licensed, long-lived generation capacity with multi-decade PPAs for a meaningful share of output, plus captive fuel logistics at Nigrie — assets that are genuinely hard to replicate (land, environmental clearances, transmission interconnection, PPA vintage). Against that: no independent market-share or competitor-benchmark data was found in any reviewed filing; the coal-mining leg of the captive-fuel-security moat is being actively surrendered; and roughly a third to a half of thermal output is merchant-exposed to power-exchange pricing and a state utility's own curtailment decisions, which the company's own MD&A describes as loss-making at the margin. No new generation capacity has been commissioned in over a decade; the only announced expansion — a ₹300 crore, 50 MW solar PV project at the Madhya Pradesh site, board-approved in August 2025 (News) — has no execution update anywhere in the AnnouncementsConv filing set covering March–July 2026, and is not mentioned in the FY26 Annual Report's outlook section at all.

The Q1FY27 profit number needs unpacking, not taking at face value

Q1FY27's reported consolidated PAT of ₹468.84 Cr (EPS ₹0.52) looks like a strong turnaround, but the filing itself flags why it isn't comparable: the company adopted the new corporate tax regime (Section 115BAA) from FY25-26 onward, which in Q1FY27 triggered a one-time reversal of ₹140.78 Cr of previously-recognised MAT credit entitlement and a ₹248.87 Cr reversal of deferred tax liability, a net one-time tax benefit of roughly ₹108 Cr that the company explicitly states makes "the tax provision for the current quarter... not comparable with previous periods" (Q1FY27 results, note 9). Separately, the quarter also booked a ₹193.63 Cr exceptional charge for the Amelia/Bandha coal-mine surrender (§01). Strip both one-offs out and the underlying quarter is far closer to Q1FY26's ₹278.13 Cr than the headline number suggests — a useful caution against reading Q1FY27 as evidence the FY26 margin problem has been fixed.

Forward margin guidance

None found in numeric form. The FY26 Annual Report's Outlook (§10) states an intent to "optimize costs and improve financial performance" and cites "lower finance cost post implementation of the Debt Resolution Plan" as a positive, but sets no target margin, cost ratio, or profit figure for FY27.

⚠
Disclosed contingent claims jumped from ₹2,116 Cr to ₹9,126 Cr in one year — the single biggest balance-sheet flag in this reportNote 44 of the FY26 audited financial statements shows "Claims against the Company not acknowledged as debts" rising from ₹2,115.94 Cr (FY25) to ₹9,126.20 Cr (FY26) — a 4.3x increase in twelve months, against a market capitalisation of ₹11,719 Cr. The overwhelming driver is an Andhra Pradesh Directorate of Mines & Geology (DMG) dispute inherited from a now-expired sand-mining sub-contract: cumulative show-cause/demand notices alleging illegal extraction reached ₹8,557.04 Cr by 31 Mar 2026 and a further ₹857.34 Cr by 30 Jun 2026, with formal demand notices (as opposed to show-cause notices) of ₹2,216.54 Cr (Mar-26) rising to ₹3,478.87 Cr (Jun-26) — the latter stayed by the Andhra Pradesh High Court, and management states the underlying sub-contracted operations were not the Company's direct liability. Separately — and this report could not confirm whether it is included in the ₹9,126 Cr figure or sits outside it — ICICI Bank (lead lender) has separately claimed ₹5,696.51 Cr in "recompense" under the 2019 Framework Agreement, which the Company disputes and management states is "presently... nothing... payable/due." Both statutory auditors, Lodha & Co LLP, issued a qualified opinion on both the FY26 annual results and the Q1FY27 review, citing non-provisioning against a further corporate guarantee to JAL's lenders (originally USD 15 million, admitted at ₹511.73 Cr by JAL's resolution professional) as one of several qualification items. This is a large, actively litigated, and only partially quantified liability overhang that sits well outside the headline P&L and balance-sheet ratios.

The 2019 restructuring's CCPS overhang is still live

₹3,805.53 Cr of Compulsorily Convertible Cumulative Preference Shares, issued to lenders as part of the 2019 Framework Agreement debt restructuring, sit on the balance sheet as "Instrument entirely equity in nature," carrying a 29-year maturity. The FY26 Annual Report states plainly that "since the CCPS shall be converted upon maturity at a rate as per prevailing SEBI Regulations at that time, the likely impact thereof cannot be worked as on today" — i.e., existing equity shareholders face an unquantified future dilution event whose scale depends on the conversion-date share price, not a fixed ratio.

No dividend

The FY26 Annual Report's Directors' Report lists "Proposed Dividend" as nil for both FY25 and FY26. Consistent with continued deleveraging priorities and the Framework Agreement's terms (which typically restrict distributions while restructured debt remains outstanding), this is unsurprising but confirms JPPOWER remains a pure capital-appreciation (or capital-preservation) proposition for equity holders, not an income one.

Earnings-quality check: passes cleanCFO has comfortably exceeded PAT in every year shown, and the conversion ratio actually improved as PAT fell (1.89x → 2.11x → 2.89x) — the opposite of the "PAT growing faster than CFO" red flag the analysis method warns against. This is typical and expected for a capital-intensive generator with heavy non-cash depreciation (₹472.80 Cr in FY26 alone) relative to a comparatively modest PAT base, and it means FY26's profit decline was not accompanied by any hidden deterioration in cash generation — the cash decline tracked the reported-earnings decline, it didn't outrun it.

Read plainly: free cash flow of ₹1,293 Cr in FY26, on a market cap of ₹11,719 Cr, is an 11% free-cash-flow yield — a genuinely attractive number in isolation, and the reason net debt keeps falling even with earnings under pressure. Financing cash flow was negative every year shown (−₹963.8 Cr, −₹891.9 Cr, −₹762.2 Cr, FY24→FY26) — consistently used to pay down debt and interest rather than fund growth capex or dividends, which is exactly what a company still working through a formal debt-restructuring framework should be doing. The open question, addressed in §10, is whether this same free-cash-flow generation continues once (if) the surrendered coal mines' compensation processes conclude, the DMG and ICICI Framework Agreement disputes in §05 are resolved one way or another, and/or Adani Power's new board redirects capital toward the diversification (solar, per §01–02) that has so far only been announced, not funded.

Other disclosure insights — SAST/SEBI filings, credit ratings

With no earnings call in this dataset, the SEBI takeover-regulation disclosures are the richest signal available on how the new ownership structure is actually financed.

Adani Power has already re-pledged 19% of JPPOWER to its own acquisition lenders

On 21 and 27 May 2026, Adani Power (through security trustee IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd) disclosed creating a fresh pledge over 1,20,05,09,431 JPPOWER equity shares (17.517% of total share capital) plus a non-disposal undertaking over a further 10,21,88,566 shares (1.49%) — together, 19.01% of JPPOWER's entire share capital, or 79.2% of Adani Power's newly-acquired 24% stake, encumbered in favour of lenders financing the acquisition. The disclosure states this is a continuation of a pre-existing pledge structure the Resolution Plan specifically preserved (the shares were already pledged by JAL to its own lenders; Adani Power "has created a fresh pledge over the Pledged Shares" rather than releasing and re-encumbering from a clean base), financing loans totalling roughly ₹13,313 Cr across several facilities (₹2,150 Cr + ₹7,315 Cr + ₹46 Cr + ₹2,258 Cr + ₹1,200 Cr + ₹500 Cr + ₹150 Cr per the Annexure-II disclosure) with a disclosed security cover ratio of just 0.16x on the largest tranche and 0.11x on the non-disposal-undertaking tranche — i.e., the pledged JPPOWER shares alone cover only a small fraction of the underlying borrowing, meaning this collateral is only one part of a much larger financing package behind the JAL acquisition, not a fully-covered pledge in isolation.

●
Reading this correctlyA high pledge ratio on newly-acquired shares is not automatically bearish for JPPOWER's own operations — it reflects how Adani Power financed the JAL acquisition, not JPPOWER's own balance sheet. But it is directly relevant to minority shareholders: it means the identity of JPPOWER's largest shareholder is itself leveraged against these very shares, and a stress event at Adani Power's acquisition-financing level (unrelated to JPPOWER's own operating performance) could in principle trigger pledge enforcement and a forced-seller dynamic in the stock. This is a standard feature of leveraged Indian holding-company acquisitions, not unique to this situation, but it is a real and disclosed risk factor.

The DMG sand-mining dispute: an inherited, escalating liability from a discontinued business

Detailed in §05, this deserves restating here as a disclosure-quality point: the underlying sand-mining contracts in Andhra Pradesh expired in May 2023 and were, per the company, entirely sub-contracted on a back-to-back basis, with the Company itself asserting it holds a "No Due Certificate" from the Directorate of Mines & Geology. Management's position has been consistent across every filing reviewed, but the escalating notice values themselves are an objective, disclosed fact regardless of which side is ultimately right.

Bottom line

What's good: genuine, verifiable operating improvement at the plant level — PLF up sharply at both thermal stations (Bina 68.6%→75.2%, Nigrie 80.9%→88.8%) and total saleable generation up 9.0% YoY; continued deleveraging with total debt down for three straight years and net debt/equity improving to 0.27x; consistently strong free cash flow (₹1,293 Cr in FY26, an 11% FCF yield on market cap) with a CFO/PAT conversion ratio that actually improved as earnings fell, a clean earnings-quality pass; and a new large, well-capitalised strategic shareholder (Adani Power) with direct sector expertise now controlling the board, which could in time bring capital discipline and growth capital this company has lacked for a decade.

What's not good: FY26 consolidated PAT fell 44.6% even as revenue and generation both grew — a cost/realisation problem the company's own disclosures trace mainly to a near-doubling of "other expenses" and a fresh receivables provision, not to financing or depreciation; every profitability ratio the company itself discloses (ROE, ROCE, net profit ratio, DSCR) declined 18–48% YoY; capex has collapsed to near-zero (₹7.9 Cr against ₹472.8 Cr of depreciation) with no funded growth plan on record; disclosed contingent claims against the company nearly quadrupled to ₹9,126 Cr in a single year, against a market cap of ₹11,719 Cr, with the statutory auditor issuing a qualified opinion in three consecutive periods on related and unrelated legacy guarantee/claim items; the coal-mining business is being surrendered outright; no dividend has been paid; a ₹3,805.53 Cr convertible-preference-share overhang from the 2019 restructuring remains unquantified in its eventual dilution impact; and 19.01% of the company's total share capital is now pledged by its new largest shareholder to finance its own acquisition, an external leverage risk unrelated to JPPOWER's own operations.

How management has navigated it: the outgoing Jaypee-era management's FY26 Annual Report frames the year's results as "continued sustain[ment] at an optimal level due to operational efficiency and lower finance cost," a characterisation this report's own tabulated ratios do not fully support given the 44.6% PAT decline. There is no earnings call or investor presentation in this dataset to test management's framing against analyst questions. The incoming Adani Power-nominated board has, in its first ~10 weeks on record (through 30 Jul 2026), delivered one clean quarterly filing (Q1FY27, albeit with the one-time tax and exceptional items flagged in §04), formalised the coal-mine surrender process, and made no public statement yet on capital allocation, the solar project, or a strategy for the DMG/ICICI legacy claims — a "wait and observe" opening posture that is itself informative, if not yet reassuring either way.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood the next 12 months resolve in the bulls' favour from today's starting price: moderate, roughly 40–50%. A credible, better-capitalised, sector-expert owner has just taken board control of a company generating genuinely strong free cash flow off an improving operating asset base — the ingredients for a re-rating exist. But this report's own numbers show FY26 profitability actually deteriorated on nearly every self-disclosed metric even before the ownership change, the balance sheet carries a near-quadrupled and only partially resolvable contingent-claims overhang close to the entire market cap, and the new owner has already leveraged 19% of the company's own shares to fund the acquisition. None of that is necessarily fatal, and the deleveraging/FCF trend gives real downside support — but the bull case here depends much more on how Adani Power resolves inherited legacy liabilities and whether it funds genuine growth capex than on anything the FY26 numbers alone would support.

●
What would move this viewWatch, in order of weight: (1) any resolution — settlement, dismissal, or crystallisation — of the DMG (₹3,479 Cr and rising) and ICICI recompense (₹5,697 Cr) claims, the single largest swing factor in this note; (2) whether a permanent MD/CEO is appointed under the new board, and what capital-allocation priorities that appointment signals; (3) whether the August 2025 solar project (or any other capex commitment) actually moves past board approval into funded execution — capex has been near-zero for a full year now; (4) receivable days and the doubtful-receivables provision trend — a further rise past FY26's 72 days, alongside another large provision, would be a genuine deterioration rather than the mild uptick seen so far; (5) whether CRISIL's Watch Negative resolves to Stable/upgrade or to a downgrade; (6) any change in Adani Power's own pledge/encumbrance level on its 24% JPPOWER stake, which is a leverage risk sitting one level above this company's own balance sheet.
