CESC Limited

NSE: CESC · BSE: 500084 · FY2023–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing, investor-presentation and annual-report synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: A 127-year-old Kolkata distribution monopoly quietly compounding through bolt-on acquisitions (Chandigarh, Feb 2025) and a newly aggressive 10 GW renewable-energy push — anchored by an August-2026 ₹4,859 crore acquisition of 1.4 GWp of ReNew's operating solar/wind assets — while FY26 headline growth (revenue +8.9%, PAT +13.2%) looks clean until you split it apart: full-year cash-flow quality genuinely improved (operating cash flow +57% against PAT +13%, and regulatory-income's share of pre-tax profit fell from 70% to 43%), but Q4FY26 alone reversed that trend, with almost two-thirds of that quarter's pre-tax profit coming from a swing in regulatory income rather than the underlying business. Group free cash flow was nearly wiped out by the capex super-cycle (₹718 Cr → ₹141 Cr in one year) even as the Board kept raising the dividend and then declared a ₹6/share interim payout in the same August filing that also reported a 3% YoY consolidated PAT print and a 3% EBITDA decline. Leverage is climbing (debt/equity 1.5x→1.7x) and one rating agency (CARE) has held a Negative Outlook through the year while two others (ICRA, India Ratings) sit at Stable — a live disagreement worth tracking, not a settled question.

01

What CESC does, and how it has grown

A 127-year-old regulated Kolkata monopoly that funds a much younger, faster-moving expansion into other cities' distribution licences and, since 2024, a systems-level push into renewable generation and manufacturing.

CESC Limited, flagship of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka (RPSG) Group, has been generating and distributing electricity in Kolkata since 1899 (MarketData/overview.md; Annual Report FY26). Its core, standalone business is an integrated utility: it distributes power across 567 sq. km of licensed area (Kolkata, Howrah, Hooghly, North & South 24 Parganas) to about 3.78–4.9 million customers, and meets roughly 82% of that demand from its own embedded generation — the 750 MW Budge Budge Generating Station (BBGS) and 135 MW Southern (Garden Reach) station, both wholly owned, plus the 600 MW Haldia plant held through wholly-owned subsidiary Haldia Energy Limited (HEL) under a long-term PPA. This embedded-generation model (own most of what you distribute) is the historical moat: it insulates the Kolkata licence from merchant power-price volatility and has let CESC push its own T&D loss down to an all-time low of 6.11% in FY26 (from 6.5% in FY25 and 8.4% as recently as FY21 — Investor Presentation, Q4 FY26).

Around that core, CESC has built three growth arms, all run through subsidiaries: (1) independent thermal & renewable generation — Dhariwal Infrastructure (600 MW, Chandrapur), Crescent Power (40 MW AFBC + 18 MW solar, Tamil Nadu) — selling to CESC Kolkata, NPCL, TANGEDCO and merchant/medium-term buyers; (2) distribution ventures outside Kolkata — licensed distribution in Greater Noida (Noida Power Company, NPCL) and Chandigarh (Chandigarh Power Distribution, CPDL, acquired February 2025), plus input-based franchisee (IBF) contracts in Kota, Bharatpur and Bikaner (Rajasthan) and Malegaon (Maharashtra); and (3) renewable energy, run through subsidiary Purvah Green Power Private Limited (PGPPL), targeting 3.2 GW by FY29 (Phase 1) and 10 GW by FY32 (Phase 2) across solar, wind, hybrid and battery storage — the newest and, based on FY26–27 newsflow, currently fastest-growing part of the group (see §03, §05).

Read plainly: EPS was essentially flat for three years (₹10.13 → ₹10.38 → ₹10.33, FY23–FY25) before a 12.6% jump in FY26. Section 03–04 show that jump was real but concentrated — driven substantially by the Chandigarh acquisition ramping (its first full year), cost/procurement savings, and a large positive swing in regulatory income, not a broad-based reacceleration across every subsidiary. Section 05 shows the CPDL deal itself, and now the much larger ReNew renewable acquisition, mark a real change in strategy: CESC has shifted from a slow, organic T&D-loss-reduction compounder into a company actively using M&A to add both distribution licences and, since August 2026, contracted renewable capacity.

The 2025–26 pivot: renewables and, more speculatively, manufacturing

Two developments mark a genuine strategic shift beyond the historical Kolkata-plus-bolt-on-DISCOMs model. First, the renewable buildout: Purvah Green had, by end-FY26, six RE projects (2,400 MW) under implementation across Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, won via competitive bidding from SECI, NPCL, REMCL and CESC Kolkata itself, plus a further 175 MW SECI wind LOA in Q1FY27; then, on 10 Aug 2026, Purvah signed a Share Purchase Agreement to acquire six operating ReNew solar/wind SPVs (1,411 MWp, >90% tied to 25-year SECI PPAs) for an enterprise value of ₹4,859 crore — the single largest capital commitment in this filing window (see §05). Second, and considerably earlier-stage: a foray into solar cell & module manufacturing (a 3 GW DCR-linked line planned for Greater Noida, with equipment-vendor finalisation and site activities underway as of the Q1FY27 deck, commissioning targeted for 2027) plus a separately disclosed, still-early Odisha in-principle approval (₹4,500 Cr, 3 GW cells / 3 GW modules / 5 GWh battery — News, 22 Jan 2026). Neither manufacturing project has a signed, binding investment commitment visible in this dataset yet; both are tracked here as intent, not delivered capacity.

The moat, and its limits

The durable part of the moat is regulatory: CESC's Kolkata distribution licence is a legal monopoly in its area, its tariffs are cost-plus (West Bengal Electricity Regulatory Commission, WBERC) with a regulatory-asset true-up mechanism, and its embedded generation gives it more control over supply cost than a pure-play discom has. The weaker, less-tested part is the newer growth engines: the IBF/DF ventures outside Kolkata compete for contract renewal and carry materially worse T&D-loss profiles (Malegaon at 36.3% FY26, still the group's largest structural laggard — see §03), and the renewable business is a capital-intensive, execution-dependent bet in a crowded, policy-driven bidding market where CESC itself acknowledges (Annual Report, Risks & Concerns) that "surplus power generation capacities expose the industry to risks associated with difficulties in executing PPAs and adverse price movements." No independent market-share or competitor-benchmark data for any segment was found in the reviewed filings; scale claims (e.g., "10 GW by 2031-32," "Fastest Growing Utility-Scale Project Developer") are company- or award-body-sourced, not independently verified here.

Geography, expansion & new order wins

Vertical expansion: the group already sells power through generation (thermal + renewables), transmission-adjacent distribution and, from 2027, potentially solar-cell/module manufacturing — a genuine attempt at backward integration into its own renewable supply chain (§01). Horizontal expansion: five distribution geographies beyond Kolkata (Greater Noida, Chandigarh, Kota, Bharatpur, Bikaner, Malegaon) plus RE project sites across Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and (via the ReNew deal) Karnataka solar/wind assets. Confirmed new order wins in this filing window: a 250 MW SECI wind LOA and a further 175 MW SECI wind LOA (Q1FY27), two new hybrid/RTC awards from CESC Kolkata and REMCL disclosed across the two decks, and the 10 Aug ReNew acquisition itself. No revenue-by-geography breakdown beyond the subsidiary table above was found; no export revenue is evidenced — all disclosed activity is domestic.

The single biggest quality flag: Q4FY26's profit growth leaned heavily on regulatory income, reversing the full-year trendFor the full year, CESC's earnings quality genuinely improved: regulatory income's share of consolidated PBT fell from 70.1% (FY25) to 42.6% (FY26) — i.e. a shrinking share of profit came from the accounting recognition of future tariff true-ups, and more came from the operating business (company's own "Profit before regulatory income and tax" rose from ₹534 Cr in FY25 to ₹1,216 Cr in FY26, a 128% increase). But Q4FY26 alone moved the opposite way: regulatory income's share of that single quarter's PBT jumped to 66.6% (from 30.2% in Q4FY25), and the standalone entity's own disclosed EPS-excluding-regulatory-income fell from ₹2.15 (Q4FY25) to just ₹0.86 (Q4FY26) — even though headline standalone EPS rose (₹1.64→₹1.69) over the same comparison, per the company's own Note 5(ii) disclosure in the financial results. In other words, the quarter that drove much of FY26's reported PAT growth was, on the company's own regulatory-income-adjusted numbers, a weaker underlying quarter than the year-ago period, masked by an accounting item. Q1FY27's regulatory-income share (33.0%) reverted back down — consistent with the full-year FY26 trend rather than Q4FY26's spike — but that quarter's EBITDA fell YoY regardless (§03), so the underlying picture stays mixed rather than clean in either direction.

What moved FY26 and Q1FY27

Full-year FY26 EBITDA margin was essentially flat (25.4%→25.3%) even as absolute EBITDA grew 9.2%, i.e. the group grew in line with its existing cost structure rather than expanding or compressing unit economics. Management's own investor-deck commentary attributes part of this to "significant savings achieved in variable cost on both fuel and power procurement" during the year (Investor Presentation, Q4 FY26 Key Highlights) — consistent with cost of fuel actually falling in absolute terms (₹3,838 Cr FY26 vs ₹4,023 Cr FY25) despite higher generation and sales volumes. Finance cost grew only modestly for the full year (+2.7%, ₹1,324→₹1,360 Cr) despite consolidated debt growing ~20% (§05) — and in both Q4FY26 and Q1FY27, finance cost actually fell YoY (₹335→₹317 Cr; ₹363→₹312 Cr) even as the balance sheet grew, likely reflecting refinancing at better terms and/or a rising share of new project debt being capitalised into construction-in-progress rather than expensed (CWIP rose from ₹403 Cr to ₹2,905 Cr consolidated — §05) rather than a lower cost of debt across the whole book. Depreciation was essentially flat to slightly down across the comparable quarters despite the capex ramp — consistent with a large share of new renewable capacity still sitting in CWIP, not yet commissioned and depreciating (§05).

Other disclosure insights

With no earnings call in this dataset, the most useful colour comes from the investor decks' own bullet commentary and one long-running regulatory dispute worth tracking.

The Sarisatoli coal-mine and WBERC true-up disputes are a recurring, unresolved theme

Both the standalone and consolidated financial-results notes (identical language, FY26 filing) disclose an ongoing, multi-year regulatory dispute: WBERC's tariff and Annual Performance Review (APR) orders for FY2018-19 through FY2025-26 have, per the company, "deviated from past practices/extant regulations in certain matters," including issues tracing back to the Sarisatoli coal mine (operational since April 2015). CESC states it has filed or is filing appeals and is "confident of the matter being adjudicated in its favour," with adjustments to be made "on the matter reaching finality." This is directly the source of the large, volatile regulatory-income line examined in §04 — it is not a one-off item but a standing, multi-year recognition process tied to litigation whose outcome is not yet final. A similar dispute is separately disclosed for subsidiary NPCL with UPERC over FY2023-24 truing-up.

Renewable manufacturing ambitions remain early-stage

The Greater Noida solar-cell/module project (3 GW, DCR-linked) progressed from "technology selection and vendor finalisation underway" (Q4FY26 deck) to "equipment vendor finalized & LLI orders in advanced stage, site activities initiated" (Q1FY27 deck) — genuine but early-stage progress, with commissioning still targeted for 2027 in both decks (i.e. no schedule change disclosed). The separately reported Odisha manufacturing MoU (₹4,500 Cr, News 22 Jan 2026) has not resurfaced in either investor deck reviewed, and its status is unclear from this dataset — flagged as a gap rather than assumed to be progressing or shelved.

BESS build-out is a real, if smaller, parallel commitment

Both decks reference execution of 1,500 MWh of battery energy storage capacity across RTC and Solar+BESS projects, plus a standalone-level 40 MW/80 MWh grid-connected BESS under installation at a CESC Kolkata substation (Annual Report) to manage renewable-power intermittency on the distribution side — a sensible complement to the generation-side RE build given the group's own growing reliance on intermittent sources.

A 22-year-old litigation matter closed, immaterially

The Calcutta High Court closed a defamation case (originally over a 2004 broadcast branding CESC a "Big Thief") on 27 Mar 2026, accepting unconditional apologies and awarding only token damages — a legacy-cleanup item with no financial materiality, included here only because it appeared in the tracked news feed.

Bottom line

What's good: a genuine, multi-year, company-wide T&D-loss-reduction track record culminating in an all-time-low 6.11% at Kolkata; a well-integrated Chandigarh acquisition already profitable and improving fast (PAT ₹4 Cr→₹9 Cr YoY in Q1); a renewable-energy platform that is, on the one hard comparison available, executing roughly in line with its own guided capex envelope (₹23,000+ Cr guided vs ~₹23,159 Cr now committed); full-year FY26 cash-flow quality that genuinely improved (CFO growth of 57% comfortably outpacing PAT growth of 13%, and regulatory income's share of PBT falling from 70% to 43%); a standalone parent whose own free cash flow more than doubled and comfortably covers its own dividend; a clean, unmodified audit opinion from an EY-network auditor; and a heavily bullish 11-analyst sell-side consensus with a mean target 36% above CMP.

What's not good: Q4FY26's much-touted 19% consolidated PAT growth leaned on a large regulatory-income swing rather than the operating business, and the company's own regulatory-income-excluded standalone EPS actually fell YoY that quarter; Q1FY27 already shows consolidated and standalone EBITDA declining YoY even as revenue grew, with Prabhudas Lilladher's own note flagging a 7% PAT miss on widening Malegaon losses; group free cash flow was compressed from ₹718 Cr to just ₹141 Cr in a single year by the capex ramp, and the FY26 consolidated dividend of ₹803 Cr — followed by a fresh ₹6/share interim payout in the same filing as the weak Q1FY27 print — was not covered by group-level free cash flow; consolidated leverage climbed from 1.5x to 1.7x debt/equity with debt-service coverage weakening on both disclosed variants, and CARE Ratings has held a Negative Outlook throughout the year while two peer agencies sit at Stable; Malegaon remains a large, only slowly improving structural loss-maker; and no earnings-call transcript exists anywhere in this dataset to check any of this against management's own explanation.

How management has navigated it: pragmatically and, on the M&A front, opportunistically — the Chandigarh and ReNew deals both add scale roughly on the terms guided, and the newly hired Head of Regulatory Affairs (28 years' sector experience) is a sensible response to how large a swing factor the WBERC/Sarisatoli regulatory-income dispute has become. Less transparently: no investor call or Q&A record exists in this dataset for management to explain the Q4FY26 regulatory-income spike, the Q1FY27 EBITDA decline, or Malegaon's stalled turnaround in its own words, and the interim dividend was announced the same day as a quarter of falling core profitability — a sequencing pattern worth watching rather than a settled concern.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood the next 12 months resolve in the bulls' favour from today's starting price: moderate-to-favourable, roughly 55–60%. The fundamentals case is more credible here than the sell-side consensus alone would suggest — full-year cash-flow quality genuinely improved and the RE platform is tracking its own capex guidance — but this report's own tabulated numbers surface a real, recent deceleration (Q1FY27 EBITDA down YoY at both standalone and consolidated levels) and a capital-allocation tension (rising dividend against collapsing group free cash flow and rising leverage) that the uniformly bullish news tone does not fully capture. None of these are disqualifying for a regulated utility mid-way through a genuine, well-telegraphed capacity build — but they are exactly the kind of items a standing analyst call would normally press management on quarter after quarter, and that pressure is not visible anywhere in this dataset.
