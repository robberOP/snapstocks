Equity Research Note · Renewable Energy IPP/EPC · Solar, Wind & Hybrid

KPI Green Energy Limited

NSE: KPIGREEN · BSE: 542323 · FY2021–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing and earnings-call synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: KPI Green has compounded revenue, EBITDA and PAT at roughly 90–100% CAGR since FY21 on the back of a captive/EPC (“CPP”) engine, and is now mid-pivot into capital-intensive, 25-year annuity Independent Power Producer (“IPP”) assets — total portfolio up from 4.06 GW to 6.94 GW in a single year. But the CMD’s public growth guidance has been cut in stages over five quarters (historically “70–80%”, then “60–70%” as of May 2025, then “40–50%” as of May 2026), and Q1 FY27 delivered just 16% revenue growth — below even the lowered bar — with PAT down 14% YoY as interest cost (+111%) and depreciation (+70%) on the newly-energised IPP fleet outran EBITDA growth (+21%). Net debt/EBITDA has more than doubled in a year, from 1.73x (FY25) to 4.39x (FY26); inventory days have also roughly doubled; the CFO resigned the same day Q1 FY27 results were filed, replaced same-day by an externally-hired successor; and the FY25 Annual Report discloses related-party sales equal to roughly 41% of standalone revenue, alongside a personal royalty to the promoter that doubled year-on-year with no stated basis. The stock trades near 52-week lows, well under both its 50- and 200-day averages, despite five straight years of accelerating absolute profit.

01

What KPI Green does, and how it has grown

A Gujarat-rooted solar/wind EPC contractor that has spent six years compounding at near-100% and is now converting itself into a 25-year annuity power producer — the single biggest strategic and financial swing in this report.

Incorporated in 2008, listed on BSE’s SME platform in 2019, migrated to the BSE/NSE Main Board in 2021, and renamed from K.P.I. Global Infrastructure Limited to KPI Green Energy Limited in 2022–2023 (sources differ on the exact year: yfinance dates the rename to April 2022, the FY25 Annual Report’s own history section to 2023 — both are the company’s own records, and this report flags rather than resolves the discrepancy), the Surat, Gujarat-headquartered company operates as an independent power producer and captive/EPC solar-wind contractor under the “Solarism” brand. It reports through two formal, statutory (Ind AS Note 50) segments — Sale of Power & Solar Power Plant (99.98% of FY25 revenue, ₹1,735.13 Cr) and Sale of Plots (₹0.32 Cr, functionally immaterial) — but management’s own internal split, used throughout its investor communication and not broken out in the audited segment note, is between IPP (Independent Power Producer: KPI builds, owns and operates solar/wind/hybrid plants and sells the power under 25-year Power Purchase Agreements, mostly to Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Limited, GUVNL) and CPP (Captive Power Producer / EPC: KPI builds plants for corporate & industrial clients — L&T, Colourtex, UPL, Tata Motors, the Indian Air Force, Coal India, Adani Green, Aditya Birla Renewables — and for utility-scale government tenders). The company is part of the wider KP Group, founded in 1994 by promoter Dr. Faruk G. Patel, who remains Chairman & Managing Director and is also MD of sister listed entity K.P. Energy Limited and a director of KP Green Engineering Limited — all three under common promoter control (FY25 Annual Report). The group states 32+ years of operating history, ~9.2 GW of group capacity, and a public 10 GW-by-2030 target (2026-08-11 investor presentation).

Every year from FY21 to FY26 delivered accelerating absolute profit, and the CAGR figures above are genuinely rare for a company of this size. But the composition of that growth has shifted hard: FY26 alone saw fixed assets more than double (₹2,524 Cr→₹5,427 Cr) and total debt roughly triple (see §05), as the company committed capital to 25-year IPP assets rather than the faster-cycling, less capital-intensive EPC/CPP business that built the earlier track record. Sections 03–06 show this transition is not yet cash-flow-neutral, and Q1 FY27 is the first quarter in this dataset where the strain shows up in PAT itself, not just the balance sheet.

The moat, such as it’s evidenced

No independent market-share or competitor-benchmark figure was found anywhere in this dataset — every scale claim (“one of India’s leading players in Gujarat,” a historical win-rate of ~75% on bids, per the CFO on the 2026-05-18 call) is self-reported. What is independently verifiable: a genuinely large and growing land bank (5,946+ acres at FY25-end per the Annual Report, up 168% YoY; 8,657 acres by Q1 FY27) and power-evacuation position (3.26+ GW at FY25-end, up from 846 MW in Mar-2023; 5.10 GW by Q1 FY27) — both classic, hard-to-replicate entry barriers for solar/wind development in India, and specifically cited by management as the two resources gating competitors’ growth, not KPI’s own; 133 operating sites; long-tenure (25-year) PPAs with GUVNL, an investment-grade Gujarat state discom counterparty; a Network Operations Centre built on IBM’s Maximo platform monitoring 200+ client sites, plus robotic panel-cleaning technology claimed at ~98% efficiency (FY25 Annual Report); a first-mover green bond structure (§05); and marquee C&I/PSU order wins (Coal India, Adani Green, Aditya Birla Renewables, MAHAGENCO). Whether this constitutes a durable moat versus simply being an early, well-capitalised entrant into a policy-driven, currently-uncrowded segment of a fast-growing but competitive sector is not something this dataset can independently settle.

Group structure and the KP Energy / KP Green Engineering relationship

KPI Green Energy Limited sits inside a wider, promoter-controlled group that runs to 50+ entities by the Annual Report’s own count, of which two — K.P. Energy Limited (KPEL), focused on wind EPC/Balance-of-Plant, and KP Green Engineering Limited — are separately listed under the same promoter, Dr. Faruk G. Patel. Management disclosed on the 2026-08-12 call that KP Energy pays KPI a 2% royalty on EPC execution value for wind/hybrid Balance-of-Plant work subcontracted to it; one investor (Anil Sarin, K16 Advisors, Q2 FY26 call) explicitly challenged this as potential “revenue double-counting” between the two listed entities, and management committed to adding an RPT-clarity slide to the following quarter’s presentation — a commitment this report could not verify was fulfilled. That 2% royalty framing turns out to understate the scale of intra-group flow considerably: the FY25 Annual Report’s related-party note shows KPI sold ₹246.75 Cr of goods/services to K.P. Energy Ltd (versus just ₹0.71 Cr in FY24) and separately purchased ₹269.93 Cr of goods/services plus ₹305.14 Cr of capital goods (a new line item, zero in FY24) from the same entity — the fuller related-party picture is developed in §07, and it is materially more significant than the royalty arrangement alone suggests. Subsidiary Sun Drops Energia Limited (BESS/battery storage, international projects) and step-down subsidiary KPGC One Private Limited (incorporated June 2026) round out the visible group structure; Sun Drops itself has been guided toward an IPO/DRHP filing every quarter since May 2025 without one materialising, and was partly diluted via a promoter-personal private placement in October 2024 (§07).

Management has stated a long-run target IPP share of 20–25% of revenue (repeated across the Q4FY25, Q1FY26 and Q1FY27 calls, though the exact target number has itself drifted between 20% and 25% across calls). Order book and geographic expansion

Management cited a CPP/EPC order book north of ₹5,000 Cr as of the Q1 FY27 call (up from ₹3,000+ Cr at the Q4 FY25 call, 1.76 GW at the time), alongside 2.88 GW of fresh capacity bookings in the trailing year. Geographic expansion beyond the Gujarat home base has been genuinely realised, not just announced: Maharashtra (MAHAGENCO, 100 MW, execution begun Q3 FY25, CEIG permission obtained Jul 2026), Rajasthan (MoU Q3 FY25, first EPC order §02 Jul 2026), plus MoUs in Odisha and Madhya Pradesh not yet converted to disclosed orders in this dataset. International expansion (Botswana — a ~5 GW government MoU with a 500 MW first phase; UAE — a data-centre/BESS project via the Fabtech Group/F Plus Healthcare alliance; a Saudi Arabia office opened per the Q4 FY25 call) remains at the MoU/early-execution stage — management itself said on the Q1 FY27 call that Botswana will contribute “no revenue this year,” i.e. FY27.

Guidance versus actual — the central finding of this report

⚠
Growth guidance has been cut at least four times across seven quarters, and the delivery gap has widened, not narrowedThe softening shows up earlier than a single headline cut. At the Q2 FY25 call (Nov 2024), the CFO gave “60% to 70%… minimum” for FY25 revenue growth. By the Q3 FY25 call (Feb 2025), the CFO’s own words already showed daylight opening between his number and the CMD’s: “I said 60% to 70% in the last [quarter] and Faruk sir said 50% to 60%… you can take 60% as a common factor in both” — the first sign, seven quarters before the more explicit cut, that the CMD’s public commitments and the CFO’s own working number were starting to diverge. By the Q4 FY25 call (19 May 2025) guidance was reaffirmed at “at least 60% to 70%” for FY26, with a PAT-margin target of 17–19%. FY26 actual: revenue grew 56% — below the guided band — while EBITDA grew 73% and PAT grew 57% (at the low end); PAT margin came in at 19%, within the guided band. At the Q4 FY26 call (12 May 2026), the CFO confirmed under direct analyst questioning (Ashish Rampuria) that guidance had been stepped down again, to “40% to 50%” for FY27 — itself down from “50% to 60%” used 1–2 quarters prior, and from the “70–80%” then “60–70%” CAGR language the CMD used historically through FY23–FY24. Management attributed this to sector-wide deceleration and, for the first time on any call in this dataset, “geopolitical conditions” affecting input costs (steel, cables, logistics) — a repeat investor on the Q1 FY27 call explicitly noted this was the first time that term had ever appeared on a KPI Green call. Q1 FY27 actual: revenue grew just 16% — a large miss even against the already-lowered 40–50% full-year guide, and the CFO admitted on the same call the company had itself “expected 20%, 30%” growth for the quarter specifically, attributing the shortfall to utility-scale billing delays with Coal India, Adani Group and Aditya Birla Group customers pushed into the next quarter. Separately, the CFO explicitly walked back the 16–18% PAT-margin guidance under repeated questioning from a self-identified long-term investor (“Subash”): FY27 PAT margin “will be lesser” than guided, with no replacement number offered.

To be fair to management: the FY26 EBITDA and PAT-margin guidance broadly held even as the revenue-growth number missed, which is a meaningfully better outcome than a broad-based miss across every line. But the pattern of the CMD’s public/media growth commitments running ahead of what the CFO can defend on calls — visible as early as Q3 FY25 and explicit by Q4 FY26 — and of the gap between guided and delivered growth widening at the same time the guided number itself keeps falling, is the single most important, well-evidenced signal in this dataset, and it should anchor how any forward guidance from this management is read going forward (see §10).

Q1 FY27’s PAT-margin collapse is real, quantifiable, and interest-driven — not a one-off accounting itemEBITDA margin actually held up fine in Q1 FY27 (36.9%, in line with the recent 35–38% range) — the deterioration is entirely below the EBITDA line. Interest cost more than doubled YoY (₹38 Cr→₹80 Cr) and depreciation rose 70% (₹30 Cr→₹51 Cr), while EBITDA itself grew only 21%. Interest coverage fell from a comfortable ~5.1–5.8x range across the prior four quarters to 3.28x in a single quarter — still a workable multiple, but a genuine, sharp step-down, not noise. Management’s explanation (newly-drawn project debt now fully expensed rather than capitalised as “interest during construction,” while the associated revenue ramps in over the following year) is mechanically plausible and consistent with the segment-mix shift in §03, but it is also exactly the kind of margin dynamic that deserves independent tracking rather than management’s own framing being taken at face value — the CFO’s own “peak interest cost” guidance was inconsistent across two calls (₹300 Cr/year at the Q4FY26 call in May 2026 vs ₹450 Cr/year at the Q1FY27 call in August 2026, for what was described as broadly the same IPP capacity build), a discrepancy this report flags rather than resolves.

“Cash profit” — a legitimate but partial counter-argument

Management’s preferred non-GAAP metric, “cash profit” (PAT + depreciation), grew a much healthier +6% YoY in Q1 FY27 (₹176 Cr vs ₹163 Cr), against PAT’s −14%. Depreciation genuinely is a non-cash charge on 25-year assets, and the argument that PAT understates near-term cash generation during a capex ramp has real merit — but it does not neutralise the interest-cost problem (a real cash cost), and it does not change the fact that PAT, not cash profit, is what determines EPS, dividend cover and the per-share value a shareholder actually owns. Both figures are presented here rather than only the one management foregrounds.

Cost-pressure commentary: EPC margins vs. sister company KP Energy

On the Q1 FY27 call, an analyst noted that KP Energy Limited’s (the separately-listed sister EPC company) EBITDA margin had fallen sharply, from 22% to 12%, versus KPI Green’s own much smaller ~300bps of margin pressure in the same period. Management’s explanation: KP Energy is a pure EPC business with no IPP-segment cost-sharing cushion, whereas ~17–18% of KPI Green’s own revenue now comes from the much higher-margin IPP segment, diluting the impact of the same input-cost inflation (steel, cables, logistics, cranes) both companies describe facing. This is a plausible, mechanically consistent explanation, and the magnitude gap (10 points vs 3 points of margin pressure) is large enough that it is worth independently verifying against KP Energy’s own filings before treating it as fully resolved — outside this report’s scope, but flagged for a reader tracking the wider KP Group.

Forward margin guidance

As noted in §03, the FY27 PAT-margin guidance of 16–18% (carried over from the earlier 17–19% FY26 guide) was explicitly walked back on the Q1 FY27 call with no replacement number given, only that “H2 will cover a portion” of the shortfall and the “full benefit” would not show until FY28. EBITDA margin guidance has been more stable: management continues to target a blended 32–37% band depending on the IPP/CPP mix (IPP EBITDA margin 85–90%, CPP/EPC 16–22%), which is broadly consistent with what has actually been delivered across all eight quarters in this dataset.

Net debt/EBITDA more than doubled in FY26, even as leverage had been genuinely improving through FY23–FY25The trend from FY23 to FY25 was a real de-risking story: net debt/EBITDA fell from 2.45x to 1.73x as the September 2024 ₹1,000 Cr QIP was partly used to repay debt (management’s own framing on the Q2 FY25 call). FY26 reversed this sharply: total debt roughly tripled (₹1,475 Cr→₹5,197 Cr) as the IPP capex programme drew down the ₹3,200 Cr SBI facility, the ₹670 Cr green bond, a ₹979 Cr Canara Bank facility and the Feb-2026 warrant issue, pushing net debt/EBITDA to 4.39x. Management’s own commentary consistently frames leverage via the debt-to-equity ratio (guided as “comfortable,” with a stated ceiling of “3:1 max” on the Q1FY27 call) rather than net debt/EBITDA — the D/E ratio benefits from the rapidly-growing equity base (QIP, warrants, retained earnings) in the denominator in a way net debt/EBITDA does not, and the two measures now tell visibly different stories about how levered the balance sheet actually is.
●
The FY25 Annual Report itself states three different debt-to-equity figures for the same yearThe front-matter highlights cite FY25 D/E of 0.33x; a KPI summary table elsewhere in the same document shows 0.43x; and the MD&A’s own statutory ratio-disclosure table (which pairs FY25 at 0.61x against FY24 at 1.05x, with the explanatory note “decreased due to increase in equity”) gives a third figure. All three appear in the company’s own audited FY25 Annual Report. This report treats the MD&A statutory table’s 0.61x as the most defensible single figure (it is the one paired with a like-for-like FY24 comparator and an explanation), but flags the inconsistency itself as a genuine disclosure-quality issue, distinct from (though similar in kind to) the FY26 net-worth basis difference noted in the coverage note above — a reader relying on any single number pulled from this company’s own materials should check which table it came from.
⚠
Standalone ROE actually declined in FY25, even as PAT grew 143%Per the FY25 Annual Report’s own MD&A ratio table, standalone return on equity fell from 13.86% (FY24) to 12.64% (FY25), and standalone operating margin compressed from 27.29% to 23.52% — both moving the wrong direction in the same year absolute PAT grew fastest (standalone PAT +143% YoY per the same report). The mechanical explanation is straightforward and not necessarily concerning on its own: the September-2024 ₹1,000 Cr QIP plus a 1:2 bonus issue expanded the equity base faster than profit grew that year, mechanically diluting ROE even as absolute returns to the business improved. But it is a concrete, sourced illustration of a pattern worth watching as the FY26 capex programme continues: rapid growth in absolute profit does not automatically mean per-unit-of-capital returns are improving, particularly for a company that has now also taken on substantially more debt (§05, above) on top of the equity dilution already seen.

This is a small, specific, easily-checked instance of a management statement on a live call being directly contradicted by the same company’s own subsequently-published audited numbers — not necessarily deliberate misstatement (FY24 numbers were provisional/unaudited at the time of the Q3 FY25 call), but the kind of concrete data point that should raise the bar for how literally other unaudited claims made on the same calls are taken, including the growth and margin guidance discussed throughout this report.

Free cash flow has been negative in every year shown (FY23–FY26), and has worsened every year as the capex programme has scaled — from −₹152 Cr in FY23 to −₹2,611 Cr in FY26. This is expected, and arguably appropriate, for a company deliberately building a 25-year annuity asset base with debt and equity raised specifically for that purpose (§05) — it is not, on its own, an earnings-quality problem in the way the FY24 CFO/PAT divergence was. But it does mean the business remains structurally dependent on external capital (debt and equity markets) rather than self-funding, and management’s own framing of cash generation on calls (CFO growth, “cash profit”) never engages with free cash flow directly — a gap in how the story is told, even if the underlying capex is genuinely productive.

Management commentary and forward cash-flow guidance

On the Q4 FY26 call, management stated that “roughly 44–45% of declared EBITDA has been delivered as cash flow” and argued that the IPP segment specifically improves cash conversion going forward because, unlike CPP/EPC, it “does not require any working capital” once operational — a reasonable structural argument, though it is in some tension with the rising inventory/receivable days documented in §05, which are driven by the CPP/EPC side of the business that still generates 83% of revenue. On the Q1 FY27 call, the CFO offered an informal, unaudited estimate of the quarter’s own operating cash flow (“PAT around ₹94 Cr plus depreciation of around ₹45 Cr, so approximately ₹140–150 Cr”) — a rough PAT-plus-D&A proxy rather than an actual cash-flow-statement figure, since no quarterly CFO statement is published or exists in this dataset.

Related-party transactions: a materially concentrated flow of business, and a doubled personal royalty to the promoter

The FY25 Annual Report’s related-party note (standalone basis, against standalone revenue of ₹1,592 Cr) discloses a scale of intra-group dealing well beyond routine: KPI sold ₹246.75 Cr of goods/services to K.P. Energy Ltd (versus just ₹0.71 Cr in FY24 — a more-than-300x jump), ₹261.88 Cr to subsidiary Sun Drops Energia, and ₹143.97 Cr to wholly-owned subsidiary KPIG Energia — combined related-party sales of roughly ₹653 Cr, or about 41% of standalone revenue. In the other direction, KPI purchased ₹269.93 Cr of goods/services plus a new ₹305.14 Cr of capital goods (zero in FY24) from K.P. Energy Ltd, and ₹141.29 Cr of capital goods from KP Green Engineering Limited — combined related-party purchases of roughly ₹716 Cr. Separately, and worth flagging on its own terms: KPI paid a royalty of ₹23.67 Cr to Chairman & promoter Dr. Faruk G. Patel personally in FY25, roughly double the ₹11.64 Cr paid in FY24; the annual report does not specify what intellectual property or asset this royalty compensates for. The company states all related-party transactions are conducted at arm’s length and in the ordinary course of business, and states that Section 188(1) of the Companies Act (requiring board/shareholder approval for certain related-party contracts) does not apply and that Form AOC-2 disclosure is accordingly not required.

⚠
This is the most significant governance flag in this reportRoughly 41% of standalone revenue flowing to related parties, a personal royalty to the promoter that doubled in a single year with no stated basis, and capital-goods purchases from a promoter-controlled sister company that went from zero to ₹305 Cr YoY are, individually and collectively, well beyond what “ordinary course of business at arm’s length” typically implies for a company of this size. None of this is necessarily improper — a genuinely integrated, common-promoter group can legitimately transact heavily with itself, and the company’s statutory auditor issued an unmodified opinion covering these transactions (below) — but the concentration and the unexplained royalty jump are exactly the kind of related-party pattern this skill’s methodology requires be surfaced explicitly rather than accepted on the company’s own “arm’s length” characterisation. Separately, the company was fined ₹5,900 by each stock exchange for a one-day-delayed related-party disclosure relating to prior periods — a minor item in isolation, but consistent with related-party disclosure being an area of demonstrated friction, not just volume.

Adding to this picture: subsidiary Sun Drops Energia’s board approved a private placement of shares to Dr. Faruk Patel personally on 11 Oct 2024, diluting KPI Green’s own stake and converting Sun Drops from a wholly-owned to a non-wholly-owned subsidiary — a second instance, alongside the §02 DEK/Mavericks acquisition (also a Sun Drops-level, promoter-linked transaction, disclosed nearly two years later), of the promoter taking a direct personal equity position inside a KPI Green subsidiary rather than at the listed-company level.

Auditor transition: a mid-year upgrade to a BDO member firm

K A Sanghavi & Co LLP served as statutory auditor through every quarter reviewed in this dataset, issuing an unmodified/clean review conclusion each time (“Emphasis of Matter — No such thing requires to be mentioned” is the standard boilerplate used every quarter without exception), and the FY25 annual audit opinion was likewise unmodified. On 11 Aug 2026, the board approved MSKC & Associates LLP, a member firm of BDO International, as the new statutory auditor for a 5-year term (through the 2031 AGM), subject to shareholder approval at the ensuing AGM. The outgoing CFO framed this explicitly as a governance-improvement step on the Q1 FY27 call (“we have added BDO, which is one of the top 5… a very positive action to increase the confidence of stakeholders… lenders and everything”), a framing this report treats as a genuine positive step rather than routine rotation, given the timing alongside the CFO transition and growing debt-investor base (the green bond, SBI facility) that would benefit from a larger-network audit signature — and, this report would add, given the scale of related-party activity a larger, more internationally-networked audit firm may be better resourced to scrutinise.

The DEK/Mavericks related-party acquisition

As detailed in §02, the 21 Aug 2026 acquisition of DEK and Mavericks Green Energy Limited by subsidiary Sun Drops Energia is explicitly disclosed as a related-party transaction because promoter Dr. Faruk G. Patel personally holds 8.95% of the target’s equity. The company states the deal was priced “at arm’s length” per an independent registered valuer’s report (Abhishek Chhajed, IBBI-registered) dated the day before the transaction was announced. Other conference-call insights worth noting

A cancelled EPC order handled transparently, an unexplained market-cap decline through Q3 FY26, and management’s own framing of why the stock has fallen even as reported profit has kept growing.

The Sai Bandhan Infinium order cancellation (Q4 FY25 call)

An investor asked directly about a BSE filing disclosing the cancellation of a 66 MW EPC order from Sai Bandhan Infinium. Management’s explanation: this was an EPC/CPP contract, not an IPP project (i.e., no long-term PPA was at risk), cancelled by KPI itself after the end-customer changed the plant design and failed to achieve financial closure for the project. This is a useful data point on execution risk in the CPP/EPC book specifically — orders can and do fall away when a customer’s own financing falls through — and management’s willingness to explain a cancellation in detail on a live call, unprompted by a scripted answer, reads as a genuine transparency positive rather than evasiveness.

Q3 FY26’s unexplained items — a real analytical gap

⚠
No management commentary exists anywhere in this dataset for Q3 FY26, because the earnings-call transcript file for that quarter is corrupted (a BSE website 404 error page saved as the transcript PDF, independently confirmed rather than assumed). Three items from that quarter have no management explanation available as a result: (1) market capitalisation fell from ₹10,646 Cr (Q1 FY26) to ₹10,090 Cr (Q2) to ₹8,733 Cr (Q3) — a ~18% decline over two quarters — despite PAT growing every quarter in the same window; (2) interest cost jumped 227% YoY in Q3 FY26 specifically, a sharper single-quarter acceleration than the surrounding quarters show; and (3) hybrid-portfolio CUF (capacity utilisation factor) dipped from 52.34% (Q2 FY26) to 48.7% (Q3 FY26), a operating-performance metric moving the wrong direction with no stated cause. None of these are necessarily red flags on their own — CUF dips seasonally, market cap moves on sentiment as much as fundamentals, and interest-cost timing depends on disbursement schedules — but the complete absence of management commentary for this specific quarter is itself a genuine coverage gap that a reader should weigh, not assume away.

Management’s own explanation for the share-price underperformance

Asked directly, twice, across two different calls (Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27) why the stock has fallen roughly 60% from its highs while reported growth has stayed strong, management’s answer has been consistent: broader “geopolitical” sector de-rating affecting the whole renewable-energy space, not a company-specific issue, plus reassurance that the promoter continues buying shares and that institutional holders remain invested (the names cited in §07). Neither call addressed the guidance-cut pattern (§03) or the leverage acceleration (§05) directly as a possible contributor to the de-rating, which this report’s own numbers suggest is at least as plausible an explanation as sector-wide sentiment.

Stock splits: four in roughly two years

The company executed stock splits in January 2023 (2:1), February 2024 (1.5:1), July 2024 (2:1) and January 2025 (1.5:1) — a cumulative ~9x increase in share count over roughly two years, purely a cosmetic/liquidity action that does not itself dilute per-share value. This is relevant context for management’s own repeated framing of the stock having delivered “100x returns” to early shareholders (Q1 FY27 call) — a claim about total value creation that is directionally supportable given the underlying profit growth in §01, but the split-adjusted share count means per-share price appreciation alone understates how much of that 100x is genuinely fundamental growth versus how retail-friendly the resulting low absolute share price has made the stock for momentum trading.

Bottom line

What’s good: a genuinely rare five-year track record — revenue, EBITDA and PAT compounding at roughly 92%, 73% and 104% CAGR respectively from FY21 to FY26, not a one-year spike; EBITDA margin has steadily, genuinely improved (29%→38% over eight quarters) even through the current cost-pressure period; a large, real, independently-verifiable capacity build (4.06 GW→6.94 GW portfolio in one year) backed by tangible land bank and power-evacuation assets, not just announcements; capex has consistently and substantially exceeded depreciation, real evidence of capacity expansion rather than financial engineering; a well-structured, well-disclosed funding programme (India’s first externally credit-enhanced green bond, a ₹3,200 Cr SBI sanction, a clean “no deviation” monitoring-agency report on warrant proceeds); consistently clean/unmodified audit opinions plus a credible upgrade to a BDO member-firm auditor; a same-day, no-vacancy CFO transition and a credibly-credentialed new Whole-Time Director with directly relevant 30GW+ sector experience; and management that, on balance, answers hard questions on calls rather than deflecting them (the Sai Bandhan cancellation, the CPP-execution-vs-guidance gap, the KP Energy margin comparison).

What’s not good: growth guidance has been cut at least four times across seven quarters (historically 70–80%, then 60–70% by May 2025 with visible internal softening as early as Feb 2025, then 40–50% by May 2026) while the actual delivery gap versus guidance has widened rather than narrowed, culminating in Q1 FY27’s 16% revenue growth against a 40–50% full-year guide and an explicit on-call walk-back of the PAT-margin target with no replacement number offered; net debt/EBITDA has more than doubled within a single year (1.73x→4.39x, FY25→FY26) even as management continues to frame leverage as “comfortable” using a debt-to-equity ratio that is flattered by a rapidly-growing equity base — and the FY25 Annual Report itself discloses three different D/E figures for the same year; interest coverage nearly halved in one quarter (5.75x→3.28x, Q4FY26→Q1FY27); standalone ROE actually declined in FY25 despite 143% PAT growth; inventory days more than doubled and debtor days have risen for three consecutive quarters, alongside decelerating rather than accelerating revenue growth; free cash flow has been negative and worsening every year since FY23, FY24 specifically showed a genuine CFO/PAT earnings-quality divergence, and a CFO statement on a Q3 FY25 call claiming positive prior-year cash flow was directly contradicted by the company’s own subsequently-published audited numbers; related-party sales ran to roughly 41% of standalone FY25 revenue, related-party purchases to roughly ₹716 Cr, and a personal royalty paid to the promoter doubled year-on-year to ₹23.67 Cr with no stated basis; the CFO resigned the same day a weak quarterly result was filed; a subsidiary IPO has been guided as imminent on every call for over a year without materialising, and that same subsidiary was separately, quietly diluted via a promoter-personal private placement; a second Sun Drops-level related-party acquisition was disclosed with a valuation report dated the day before announcement; and a director re-appointment drew only 51.52% institutional support — a specific, quantifiable governance caution signal, not vague sentiment.

How management has navigated it: transparently on individual operating items (order cancellations, margin comparisons, working-capital drivers all get direct, detailed answers on calls) but less convincingly on the two patterns that matter most — the CMD’s public growth commitments have consistently run ahead of what the CFO can defend on calls, and the scale of related-party dealing disclosed in the annual report is nowhere addressed on any earnings call reviewed for this report, despite direct investor questions about inter-group revenue flows that management answered narrowly (the KP Energy royalty specifically) without ever surfacing the much larger ₹653 Cr/₹716 Cr sales/purchase flows or the doubled promoter royalty. Neither of the two most-asked questions across every call in this dataset — why has the stock fallen so much, and when will guidance actually be met — has been answered with anything more specific than “geopolitical conditions” and reassurance. The CFO who built and explained the FY21–FY26 track record (Salim Yahoo) exited at exactly the point this pattern became most visible, handing the explaining-it-going-forward job to a CFO with no track record on this specific story yet.
