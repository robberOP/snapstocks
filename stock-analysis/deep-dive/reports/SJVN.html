SJVN Limited

NSE: SJVN · BSE: 533206 · FY2023–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing and market-data synthesis, as of 23 August 2026.

The one-line story: FY2025–26 was SJVN's most operationally active year ever — a record 13,302 million units generated (+25% YoY), three major commissionings (Buxar Thermal Unit-1, 1,000 MW Bikaner Solar, Dhubri Solar), and a one-off ₹679 crore tariff truing-up order that pushed consolidated revenue up 47% — yet consolidated profit after tax fell 21.5%, because the new thermal and solar assets are still losing money in their stabilisation phase (subsidiaries SGEL and STPL together lost over ₹350 crore) and the company booked a ₹236 crore impairment after the government reassigned its 3,097 MW Etalin project to a sister PSU. Leverage has roughly doubled in four years (consolidated debt/equity 1.91x→2.26x in FY26 alone) to fund a still-aggressive capex programme, while the auditor's own report flags SJVN as non-compliant with SEBI's independent-director and woman-director board-composition rules, and the CMD and CFO seats have both spent most of the past two years on "additional charge" from sister PSUs rather than permanent appointments. Management has itself walked back its long-standing 25,000 MW-by-2030 target as under review. The stock is down roughly 35% from its 52-week high and trades below both its 50- and 200-day averages.

01

What SJVN does, and how it has grown

A Himalayan hydropower generator turned diversified power PSU — hydro, wind, solar and now thermal — expanding fast into new technologies while its legacy hydro fleet, already running near maturity, contributes little further growth on its own.

SJVN Limited (formerly Satluj Jal Vidyut Nigam Limited, renamed 2009) is a Navratna Central Public Sector Enterprise incorporated in 1988 as a joint venture between the Government of India and the Government of Himachal Pradesh, headquartered in Shimla. Its core business, and still the source of most of its cash flow, is regulated hydropower: the 1,500 MW Nathpa Jhakri Hydro Power Station (NJHPS, its flagship, commissioned decades ago and now past 150 billion cumulative units generated) and the 412 MW Rampur Hydro Power Station (RHPS), both in Himachal Pradesh, plus the newer 60 MW Naitwar Mori station in Uttarakhand. Tariffs on these regulated assets are set by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) under an Annual Fixed Charge formula — return on equity, depreciation, interest on loans, O&M and working-capital interest — which gives predictable, cost-plus economics but also caps upside on the legacy fleet. FY26 generation from NJHPS (7,506.62 MU) and RHPS (2,108 MU) grew only 1.1% and 1.6% YoY respectively — both stations are already running near their practical ceiling, so essentially all of the company's forward growth now has to come from new capacity, not the legacy hydro base.

That new capacity has diversified rapidly. SJVN now also operates the 900 MW Arun-3 hydro project under construction in Nepal (targeting commissioning of all four units by December 2028 per the FY26 earnings call), two wind assets (Khirvire 47.6 MW, Sadla 50 MW), a growing solar portfolio (Charanka, Parasan, Gurhah, Gujrai, Raghanesda, Omkareshwar floating, and the newly commissioned 1,000 MW Bikaner and 70 MW Dhubri/Assam plants), and — its newest and largest single bet — the 1,320 MW Buxar Thermal Power Project in Bihar, whose first 660 MW unit began commercial operation on 14 November 2025. Total installed capacity stood at 4,196.5 MW across 15 operational projects plus two transmission lines as of 31 March 2026, after FY26's record 1,730 MW of capacity additions (1,070 MW solar, 660 MW thermal). The business remains formally a single reportable segment ("Generation and sale of power") under Ind AS-108, so no company-disclosed revenue-by-technology split exists; this report reconstructs the story instead from subsidiary-level disclosures made on the earnings call (see §04).

A second, capital-light business: Renewable Energy Implementing Agency (REIA)

Beyond its own generation assets, SJVN also acts as a bid-implementing agency for renewable tenders on behalf of the government — awarding and then re-selling capacity to third-party developers, similar to SECI's model. Management disclosed on the FY26 call that SJVN has awarded roughly 16 GW under this programme cumulatively, of which only about 6 GW (≈38%) had converted to signed Power Sale Agreements as of May 2026, with management candidly acknowledging that renewable tendering has slowed industry-wide (see §08).

Growth targets: a public walk-back

SJVN's stated mission — repeated in the FY26 Annual Report as recently as August 2026 — is to reach 25,000 MW of installed capacity by 2030 and 50,000+ MW by 2040. But on the 15 May 2026 earnings call, CMD Bhupender Gupta told analysts directly that this target is "being reviewed" because "due to one or the other reason… the projects did not come in time," citing delayed CPSU/VGF renewable-tender schemes and losses on already-commissioned projects running below design CUF, and said a revised, "realistic" business plan would be published "very soon." As of this report's coverage window (through 12 Aug 2026), no revised target had yet been filed — a real discrepancy between the written mission statement still circulating in the FY26 annual report and management's own verbal admission nine months of the same fiscal year prior that the number isn't credible any more.

The moat, such as it's evidenced

SJVN's most durable advantages are structural rather than competitive: regulated, cost-plus tariffs on its hydro fleet provide unusually stable, contracted cash flow (subject to periodic CERC tariff-order timing risk — see §05); Navratna/CPSE status gives it privileged access to government-allotted hydro sites (e.g. the three Himachal Pradesh projects discussed in §05 and §07) and to bilateral state PPAs (658 MW signed with Gujarat in June 2026); and its REIA role gives it deal flow without full balance-sheet exposure. Against this, capacity allocation is ultimately at the discretion of the central and state governments rather than SJVN's own strategy — illustrated starkly by the Ministry of Power's 2026 decision to reassign SJVN's entire 3,097 MW Etalin Hydro Electric Project to sister PSU NHPC Limited (§05, §08), a project SJVN had already spent on and had to write off. No independent market-share or competitor-benchmarking data was found in any reviewed filing.

EBITDA grew 26%, PAT fell 22% — the gap is almost entirely new-subsidiary losses plus a one-off impairmentManagement's own explanation, given in the Chairman's Statement and repeated on the earnings call: SJVN Green Energy Ltd (SGEL, the solar/wind subsidiary) posted a FY26 loss of ₹258 crore (widened from ₹151 crore in FY25) on revenue that more than doubled to ₹510 crore, as several plants ran below design CUF and flagship 1,000 MW Bikaner Solar had not yet reached full ramp; SJVN Thermal Pvt Ltd (Buxar Unit-1, only 3 months of FY26 operation) posted a ₹93 crore total comprehensive loss on ₹522 crore of revenue, running at ≈60% plant load factor against a ≈70% breakeven threshold typical for thermal plants. On top of that, a ₹235.86 crore consolidated impairment (Ind AS 36) was booked in Q4FY26, driven substantially by the Etalin HEP reassignment (§01, §08). Finance costs also rose sharply (consolidated finance cost +74.6% YoY, ₹743.37 Cr→₹1,297.86 Cr) as debt funded the capex programme (§05) — roughly ₹206 crore of the increase was attributed to foreign-exchange translation on the company's dollar- and yen-denominated borrowings. Management's own guidance is that both Buxar and SGEL should turn profitable in FY27 as plant availability/CUF ramps — a claim worth tracking given the curtailment issues (§08) that have already pushed some of that ramp-up into late-2026.

Standalone vs. consolidated: a genuine divergence

SJVN's standalone (parent-only, legacy-hydro-dominated) profitability actually improved in FY26: standalone PAT grew 3.9% (₹970.18 Cr→₹1,007.90 Cr), standalone Return on Capital Employed rose from 8.14% to 10.08%, and standalone operating margin held up (69.81%→71.67%). The entire consolidated profit decline is therefore attributable to the newer subsidiaries and the impairment — a useful diagnostic: the core, mature hydro business is performing fine on its own terms; the drag is squarely in the growth assets still being brought online.

Quarterly margin volatility

Company-filed operating margin (EBIT/revenue from operations) swung from 2.56% (Q4FY25, weak season) to 68.32% (Q1FY26, peak hydro season) to 61.52% (Q3FY26) to negative territory implied by the Q4FY26 net loss (31.35% operating margin but a net loss after finance costs and tax) and back to 48.17% (Q1FY27) — a roughly 66-percentage-point round trip inside five quarters. Some of this is normal hydro seasonality (Q1–Q2, the monsoon months, are structurally the strongest generation quarters); some of it is genuinely one-off (the truing-up order and the impairment both landed in Q4FY26 specifically).

Forward margin guidance

No formal numeric margin guidance was found in either the FY26 call transcript or the Q1FY27 filing — consistent with CPSE practice of not issuing forward EPS/margin guidance the way private-sector companies do. The only forward-looking margin signal is qualitative: management's stated expectation that Buxar and SGEL become profitable during FY27, which this report treats as a claim to verify against Q2–Q4FY27 results rather than a number to build a forecast on.

Contingent liabilities: a growing parent guarantee for a subsidiary's Nepal project

Contingent liabilities rose 39% to ₹5,707.05 Cr (FY26) from ₹4,104.57 Cr (FY25). The single largest component and the entire increase's driver is a corporate guarantee the parent has extended to lenders of SJVN Arun-3 Power Development Company Pvt Ltd (the Nepal hydro subsidiary), which rose to ₹4,442.24 Cr from ₹3,421.41 Cr. Management states it does not expect any probable outflow given the subsidiary's asset cover, but this is a real, growing, and directly execution-linked off-balance-sheet exposure of the parent to a foreign hydro project that has already experienced delays (unexpected geological conditions on the dam's right bank, per the May-2026 call, since addressed) and now targets commissioning of all four units only by December 2028.

A live dispute with the Himachal Pradesh state government over three hydro projects

Three hydro projects — 210 MW Luhri Stage-I, 382 MW Sunni Dam and 66 MW Dhaulasidh — were allotted to SJVN by the Government of Himachal Pradesh via MoU, but the formal Implementation Agreement has never been signed. SJVN began construction anyway to avoid time/cost overruns; on 22 April 2025 the state government indicated its intent to consider taking back these projects, and appointed an evaluator. SJVN has petitioned the Himachal Pradesh High Court, which has directed the state government not to take coercive action pending resolution, but the matter remains unresolved as of the FY26 Annual Report (filed August 2026). Expenditure already recognised on these three projects — ₹864.65 Cr in PP&E/intangibles and ₹3,068.49 Cr in capital work-in-progress as of March 2026 — sits on the balance sheet pending that resolution. This is a direct, quantified illustration of the two-government-shareholder structure creating real friction: SJVN's home state can, and in this instance has, moved to reclaim allocated capacity.

Read plainly: free cash flow has been negative in every year shown (FY23–FY26), narrowing gradually (−₹5,237 Cr→−₹3,839 Cr) but still deeply negative, funded entirely by fresh borrowings rather than any equity issuance (§05). Until CFO durably exceeds capex — not yet close, even in FY26's best year on this dataset — the company will keep needing external (debt) funding for its growth programme, and DPE-mandated dividend payouts (§07) further limit how much of standalone profit can be retained to close that gap internally.

PSU ownership: what it means for this stock specifically

Two structural implications follow directly from SJVN's dual-government ownership. First, dividend policy is DPE-directed and consistently high: FY26's total dividend of ₹1.50/share (₹1.15 interim + ₹0.35 final) implies a payout ratio of roughly 83% against standalone PAT (MarketData), leaving little internally generated capital to fund the debt-heavy capex programme documented in §05–06. Second, having two government shareholders (Centre and Himachal Pradesh) with occasionally divergent interests is not merely theoretical — it has produced the live Luhri-I/Sunni Dam/Dhaulasidh dispute (§05) and the separate Himachal Pradesh land-revenue levy litigation, both real and quantified exposures rather than abstract risks.

08

Other earnings-call insights worth flagging

A large hydro project lost to a sister PSU, a renewable bid-agency conversion rate under 40%, and near-term curtailment on already-commissioned solar assets.

Etalin HEP (3,097 MW) reassigned to NHPC

The Ministry of Power reassigned SJVN's entire 3,097 MW Etalin Hydro Electric Project in Arunachal Pradesh to sister PSU NHPC Limited, terminating the MoA SJVN held with the Arunachal Pradesh government. SJVN classified the related ₹736.97 Cr of assets and ₹0.93 Cr of liabilities as held-for-sale and booked a related impairment (§04). This is a direct, material illustration that SJVN's project pipeline is ultimately allocated at the central government's discretion, not solely a function of SJVN's own strategy or execution — the same government that also (via the Ministry of Power) appoints its CMD on additional charge.

REIA conversion rate: only ≈38% of awarded capacity has signed PPAs

Of the roughly 16 GW SJVN has awarded as Renewable Energy Implementing Agency, only about 6 GW (≈38%) had converted to signed Power Sale Agreements as of the May-2026 call. Management's own explanation is candid: DISCOMs already have 25–30% of near-term capacity needs tied up across all implementing agencies (SECI, NTPC, NHPC as well as SJVN), some tendered configurations no longer match DISCOM requirements, and newer tenders are pricing at ₹2.8–2.9/unit versus ₹2.53–2.54/unit on some older, unconverted tranches — a genuine demand-side uncertainty for this part of the business, not an execution failure on SJVN's part.

Curtailment on newly commissioned solar assets

Management disclosed on the call that the 1,000 MW Bikaner Solar plant is being curtailed by roughly 120 MW pending installation of grid-stabilisation reactor equipment (delayed to October 2026 due to limited equipment manufacturers), and flagged a further potential ≈300 MW curtailment risk at upcoming Khawada (Gujarat) capacity pending transmission-line connectivity delays. These are near-term execution frictions directly relevant to how quickly the FY27 "Buxar and SGEL turn profitable" guidance (§04) can actually be delivered.

PAF-based incentive income turned negative in Q4FY26

Management disclosed that Plant Availability Factor-based incentive income was down 40% YoY in Q4FY26 even as secondary-energy incentive income rose 16% — a granular signal that availability-linked incentive revenue (a real, if usually small, component of regulated hydro economics) came under some pressure in the same quarter as the impairment and subsidiary losses.

Bottom line

What's good: FY26 was a genuine operational record — 13,302 MU generated (+25% YoY), three major commissionings (Buxar Unit-1, 1,000 MW Bikaner Solar, Dhubri Solar), 1,730 MW of capacity added in a single year, and consolidated EBITDA up 26%; standalone (parent-level, legacy-hydro) profitability actually improved, with ROCE rising from 8.14% to 10.08%; new long-dated PPAs (658 MW with Gujarat, plus the 75 MW Jamui fixed-tariff solar project) add multi-decade revenue visibility; capex has consistently and substantially exceeded depreciation every year since FY23, real evidence of capacity expansion; credit ratings remain stable investment-grade (CRISIL/CARE AA+) with no downgrade; and the auditor's opinion on the financial statements themselves is clean and unmodified.

What's not good: consolidated PAT fell 21.5% in FY26 despite the EBITDA growth, driven by widening losses at two newly commissioned businesses (SGEL, Buxar Thermal) and a ₹236 Cr impairment tied to losing the Etalin project to a sister PSU; debt/equity jumped to 2.26x and free cash flow has been negative every year on record, funded entirely by fresh debt with zero equity issuance; a ₹1,454 Cr swing into receivables means a real share of FY26's headline revenue growth was a non-cash regulatory recognition rather than fresh cash-generative demand; contingent liabilities rose 39% on a growing parent guarantee for the Nepal subsidiary; a live dispute with the Himachal Pradesh state government clouds ₹3,933 Cr of recognised spend on three home-state hydro projects; the CMD and CFO/Director-Finance roles have both spent most of the past two years on "additional charge" from sister PSUs rather than permanent appointments; the company's own auditor flags non-compliance with SEBI's independent-director and woman-director board-composition rules; and the stock is down roughly 30% over the past 19 months, trading below both its 50- and 200-day moving averages, against a thin, recently-more-cautious two-analyst coverage universe.

How management has navigated it: candidly on the numbers — the earnings call directly and specifically explained the SGEL/Buxar losses, the receivables composition, and the finance-cost increase, rather than glossing over them, and management proactively walked back its own 25,000 MW-by-2030 target rather than letting it stand uncorrected. Less reassuringly on structure: the CMD and finance-leadership seats have been filled by additional-charge appointments from sister PSUs for most of the review period rather than dedicated hires, and the board-composition compliance gap has evidently persisted long enough for the auditor to flag it formally in the FY26 report.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood the next 12 months resolve in the bulls' favour from today's starting price: roughly 40–50%, a genuine coin-flip tilted slightly negative. The underlying regulated-hydro business is sound and standalone profitability is actually improving, and management has been unusually candid about what went wrong at the subsidiary level in FY26 — but the consolidated numbers, the leverage trajectory, the governance-appointment structure, and a currently unresolved state-government dispute over part of the core hydro pipeline are all real, quantified drags that have to clear before the forward P/E gap (37.75x trailing vs 13.75x forward) can close in the market's favour rather than the trailing multiple simply catching down to reality.
