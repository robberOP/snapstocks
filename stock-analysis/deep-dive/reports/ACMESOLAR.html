Equity Research Note · Renewable Energy / Independent Power Producer — Solar, Wind, FDRE & Battery Storage

ACME Solar Holdings Limited

NSE: ACMESOLAR · BSE: 544283 · FY2023–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing and earnings-call synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: ACME Solar has turned a rough legacy receivables book (181 days of sales outstanding in FY23) into a 14–20-day cash-discount machine by shifting almost entirely to central offtakers, while becoming the earliest and largest merchant battery-storage (BESS) operator in India — management claims roughly 40% of the country's commissioned BESS capacity — capturing peak-power tariff arbitrage at 80–82% EBITDA margins on top of an 88–92%-margin core solar/wind book. Revenue from operations grew 44% in FY26 and 63% YoY again in Q1FY27, and BESS commissioning guidance has been raised, not cut, twice in three months. But the growth is expensive and imperfectly cash-backed: FY26 operating cash flow fell 19% even as PAT nearly doubled, 71% of FY26 pre-tax profit came from treasury/DSRA interest rather than power sales, net debt more than doubled to ₹16,757 Cr before a ₹2,763 Cr June-2026 QIP diluted existing holders by roughly 16.5%, and the stock's most senior finance and R&D executives both departed within a seven-week window even as a BESS facility fire made national headlines in July. At 41x trailing earnings and near a 52-week high, this is a real execution story still being asked to prove it can fund itself.

01

What ACME Solar does, and how it has grown

A pure-play renewable IPP that has spent the past two years compounding capacity, then spent the past two quarters compounding a second, higher-margin business — battery storage — on top of it.

ACME Solar Holdings Limited (formerly ACME Solar Holdings Private Limited, CIN L40106HR2015PLC102129) is a Gurugram-headquartered independent power producer that develops, builds, owns and operates utility-scale grid-connected solar, wind, hybrid and Firm Dispatchable Renewable Energy (FDRE) projects across India, with an in-house EPC and O&M division that constructs and maintains its own plants. It is a subsidiary of ACME Cleantech Solutions Limited (promoter group holding 71.40% as of the Q1FY27 investor presentation), and its Chairman & Managing Director, Manoj Kumar Upadhyay, is the ACME Group founder. Note: the yfinance business-summary field describes an operational base of "2,540 MW" — that figure is stale; the company's own Q1FY27 disclosure puts operational contracted capacity at 2,990 MW, and this report uses the company's own current figures throughout.

Total contracted+pipeline portfolio stands at 8,070 MW (~14 GWp of generation plus ~20 GWh of BESS as currently configured), of which 6,870 MW now has a signed PPA. Management's own long-range target, restated in the Jul-26 investor presentation, is 10 GW generation capacity plus 25 GWh of BESS by 2030 — framed as a 35% capacity CAGR from today's base — and on the Q1FY27 call management said it "contemplates increasing our target beyond 10 gigawatt," i.e. the 2030 ambition itself may be revised up further, not down.

The BESS pivot: the real story of the last two quarters

Historically ACME was a conventional solar/wind IPP selling fixed-tariff power under 25-year PPAs to state and central offtakers. Starting in FY26, the company began an aggressive early-deployment strategy for battery storage: installing batteries at already-energised substations ahead of the associated solar plant's own grid connectivity, and using them to arbitrage the spread between cheap daytime/off-peak power and expensive evening peak power on the merchant/short-term market (DEEP portal, HP-TAM). Commissioned BESS capacity went from essentially nil to ~2.3 GWh by the FY26 year-end (May-26 call) to ~3.62 GWh by Q1FY27 (Aug-26 call) — management's own claim is that this represents roughly 40% of India's total cumulative commissioned BESS capacity, "one of the highest in the country." This is a self-reported figure with no independent market-share data found in this dataset, but it is directionally consistent with the scale of ACME's own commissioning relative to a nascent national base (management itself notes "whatever capacity is there in India has come up in the last 6 months").

Moat, such as it is evidenced

Three things are independently checkable from the filings: (1) a portfolio overwhelmingly connected to the Central Transmission Utility (CTU) grid rather than state grids — management describes this as a deliberate design choice that shields the company from state-level curtailment risk and gives it a regulatory-protected payment mechanism, with only a legacy Gujarat wind asset and one small 550 MWh Andhra Pradesh project on state infrastructure; (2) an offtaker book concentrated with central, government-backed counterparties (SECI, NTPC, SJVN, NHPC) at 84% of the total portfolio, versus 67% of the currently-operational book — explicitly cited by management as the driver of the DSO collapse discussed in §05; and (3) in-house EPC/O&M capability, which management describes using on the Q1FY27 call as the mechanism for a genuinely clever capital-efficiency tactic — deliberately deferring module/solar-side capex at sites where grid connectivity is delayed, installing only the battery and non-module balance-of-plant early, and capturing merchant BESS revenue while avoiding interest-during-construction drag on undeployed solar capex (see §08). None of this is independently benchmarked against competitors in this dataset (no named-competitor comparison, market-share table, or third-party ranking was found) — every scale and "leading" claim here traces to management's own disclosure.

Segment mix: BESS is now a meaningful, high-margin second engine

Of Q1FY27's ₹954 Cr total revenue, ₹226 Cr (23.7%) came from BESS power sales — 85% from short-term contracts, 15% from pure merchant sales — against a BESS cost of power of ₹39 Cr, yielding an 82% BESS EBITDA margin (management notes this is lower than the core RE portfolio's 88–91% because purchased charging power sits in operating expenses). Management disclosed short-term BESS contracts locking in ₹1,400+ Cr of revenue for partial FY27 BESS capacity, describing roughly 70–80% of the targeted 10 GWh as already contracted, with an explicit "upward bias" as the remaining ~20% gets tied up. Pressed by analysts on durability, management would only underwrite this ₹1,400 Cr+ run-rate for "the next 2, 3 years" based on its own demand-supply modelling (see §08), not longer — a genuinely candid, non-promotional answer worth noting.

Offtaker mix and geography

The operational portfolio's offtaker split is 67% central (SECI/NTPC/SJVN/NHPC), 5% GUVNL, 28% other state DISCOMs; the total 8,070 MW portfolio's split is far more central-weighted at 84%/2%/14% — i.e. every incremental MW being added skews further toward central, government-backed counterparties, which management explicitly ties to the DSO improvement in §05. Geographically the operational book remains heavily Rajasthan-concentrated (2,250 MW of the 2,990 MW operational total, or ~75%, per the operational-portfolio project table), with smaller legacy assets in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka and Telangana. Under-construction projects add exposure to more states (Rajasthan again, plus new sites tied to specific CTU substations), but the near-term revenue base stays Rajasthan-heavy, which is also where the bulk of the country's curtailment risk on state-connected infrastructure sits (though ACME's own CTU-first connectivity strategy is designed specifically to avoid that — see §01).

Quarterly margin drivers

EBITDA margin (company basis, incl. other income) has stayed in a tight 87–92% band across every quarter shown in §03, with the Q1FY27 dip to 87.1% explained entirely by mix: BESS runs at an 82% margin (purchased charging power sits in opex) against 88–91% for the core RE book, so a rising BESS revenue share mechanically pulls the blend down even as both underlying businesses are performing well. Management's own forward framing, given directly in response to an analyst question on the Aug-26 call, is a core (ex-BESS) EBITDA margin band of 88–92%, seasonally softer in the September quarter historically (~88–89%), with BESS margin guided at 80–82%. PAT margin has been more volatile quarter to quarter (18.4% in Q3FY26 to 24.6% in Q1FY27) reflecting the finance-cost and depreciation drag from the capex ramp (see below) moving faster than revenue in some quarters.

Below the EBITDA line: finance cost and depreciation are both scaling with the balance sheet

Finance costs rose from ₹233 Cr (Q1FY26) to ₹344 Cr (Q1FY27), up 47.6% YoY; depreciation rose from ₹108 Cr to ₹155 Cr, up 43.5% YoY — both tracking the debt-funded capex build documented in §05, and both growing faster than EBITDA's Q1FY27 YoY growth of 56.5%, though slower than PAT's headline 79.9% growth (which benefited from a lower effective tax drag this quarter and other-income timing). On the May-26 call, management disclosed the battery-specific slice of this: ~₹32 Cr of finance cost and ~₹27 Cr of depreciation in that quarter tied specifically to newly commissioned BESS assets, depreciated on a 20-year policy.

⚠
Other income is a large, and in FY26 dominant, share of pre-tax profitFY26 other income was ₹483.70 Cr against a PBT of ₹676.82 Cr — 71.4% of pre-tax profit. Management describes this as "primarily comprises recurring interest income from cash generated from power sales at SPV until it is upstream to ASHL," plus recurring interest on DSRA (debt-service reserve account) balances required by lender covenants — i.e., genuinely recurring treasury income on cash the group is contractually required to hold, not a one-off. Even so, it is non-power-sale income, and its scale means a meaningful share of FY26's headline profit growth reflects large cash balances (built up from the Nov-2024 IPO and heavy project-debt draws) earning interest, not core electricity-sale economics. Debt, leverage and the June-2026 QIP

Total debt more than doubled in FY26 (₹10,976 Cr→₹19,896 Cr, +81.3%), funding the sharp capex step-up (§06). ACME closed a Qualified Institutions Placement on 1–4 Jun 2026 at a floor price of ₹294.13/share (issued at ₹279.50, a ~5% discount within the permitted band), raising ₹2,762.9 Cr net via 10.02 Cr new shares — roughly 16.5% dilution against the pre-QIP ~60.6 Cr shares outstanding. The stated purpose was 76% debt repayment / 24% general corporate purposes. As of the Q1FY27 financial results (30 Jun 2026), only ₹411.0 Cr of the ₹2,100 Cr debt-repayment object had actually been utilised — 19.6% — while the general-corporate-purposes object was 99.6% utilised (₹660 Cr of ₹663 Cr). Net debt/TTM EBITDA improved from 4.2x (Q1FY26) to 2.9x (Q1FY27) and net debt/net worth from 1.7x to 1.6x, but the QIP inflow is explicitly cited by the company's own investor presentation as the driver of both improvements — not organic EBITDA catch-up to the FY26 debt build. Cash RoE fell from 23.0% to 17.3% over the same period for the identical reason (larger equity denominator) — a mechanical near-term ROE dilution from delevering that investors should not mistake for a deterioration in underlying returns.

⚠
QIP debt-repayment proceeds are being deployed much slower than the capex guidance implies urgency forOnly 19.6% of the ₹2,100 Cr debt-repayment object was used in the QIP's first month on the books. This is not necessarily a red flag on its own (large debt repayments are often timed to specific facility maturities or prepayment windows), but it sits in tension with a FY27 capex guidance of ₹15,000–20,000 Cr that implies the company needs every lever — debt paydown, fresh borrowing, and internal accruals — moving in parallel and on schedule. Worth checking again at the Q2FY27 results.

Debt vs. EBITDA growth, and the book-value data gap

FY25→FY26 total debt grew 81.3% while normalized EBITDA grew a slower 61.2% (₹1,405.54 Cr→₹2,265.04 Cr) — leverage genuinely outran earnings power on an absolute basis during FY26, even though the post-QIP ratio improved by Q1FY27 as noted above. Separately: MarketData's own "Book Value / Share" field (₹83.56, used in the §10 P/B multiple) is computed from the pre-QIP Mar-26 balance sheet (₹5,061.48 Cr net worth ÷ 60.54 Cr shares ≈ ₹83.6, an exact match) — it has not yet been updated for the June-2026 QIP. Using the Q1FY27 investor-presentation net worth of ₹8,067 Cr against ~70.6 Cr post-QIP shares outstanding implies a current book value closer to ₹114/share, and a correspondingly lower current P/B of roughly 3.5x rather than the stale 4.81x figure — flagged explicitly in §10.

Credit ratings and financing access

Group-level ratings of AA-/Stable have been re-affirmed by both ICRA (Apr-26) and CRISIL (Jan-26). Project-level ratings vary with maturity: the operational 300 MW Heergarh project carries ICRA AA-; 150 MW of operational wind assets (Pokhran, Eco Clean) carry CRISIL A+; the under-construction 150 MW Platinum Urja FDRE project carries ICRA A-. Other call insights worth watching

A capital-efficiency tactic worth flagging as a genuine strength, a candid limit on how far management will underwrite the merchant-BESS story, and a regulatory backdrop that keeps tilting in ACME's favour.

Deliberate capex deferral to protect returns

On the May-26 call, management described explicitly holding back module procurement (and the associated interest-during-construction cost) at sites where grid connectivity (LTOA/GNA) is delayed by the transmission utility, while still energising the battery and non-module balance-of-plant early to capture merchant BESS revenue in the interim. CEO Nikhil Dhingra: "we are deferring the capex also on the solar side... there is no reason to do a solar capex until your LTOA is operational... which is helping us improve our returns." This is a genuinely sophisticated capital-discipline behaviour, not just a growth narrative — worth crediting as a real operating strength.

Management's own limit on the merchant-BESS story

Repeatedly pressed by analysts on how long the current BESS tariff arbitrage (management cited realizations of ₹8–10/unit against a purchase cost near ₹2/unit) can persist, management's answer was consistently bounded: confident for "the next 2, 3 years" based on its own demand-supply modelling (an estimated 200–500 GWh of national peak-power storage need against a currently tiny installed base), explicitly declining to forecast further out and noting that competitors (implicitly Adani and others) are already replicating the same early-BESS strategy. This is a credible, non-promotional answer that also functions as its own risk disclosure: the highest-margin part of the current growth story has a management-acknowledged shelf life.

Regulatory tailwinds, catalogued across both calls

  • SECI designated sole Renewable Energy Implementing Agency (REIA) by MNRE, intended to streamline and speed up the bid-to-PPA cycle industry-wide.
  • MoP extended graded ISTS-charge waiver protection (Jul-25 to Jun-28) for projects delayed by transmission unreadiness, for PPAs signed by 30 Jun 2026 — direct financial-risk mitigation for ACME's own delayed-substation exposure.
  • CERC introduced a free-exit/merchant-conversion option for LOA holders without a signed PPA within 12 months — management frames this as net-positive for ACME (most of its own LOAs are already converted to signed PPAs, so it stands to gain from connectivity freed up elsewhere).
  • CTU began granting BESS connectivity under a Right-of-First-Refusal route, and CEA mandated grid-forming inverters for BESS projects — both raise the bar for scale and capital access, which management frames (self-servingly, but plausibly) as favouring large, well-capitalised incumbents like ACME over smaller entrants.

Currency hedging on battery procurement

Management disclosed more than $300 million hedged against dollar-denominated battery procurement, with locked-in rates around $89–93/kWh-equivalent, insulating the cost base of its 15+ GWh of ordered battery supply (from CATL and Hithium, among others) from further rupee depreciation — a specific, quantified risk-management disclosure rather than a generic reassurance.

Data centre demand: acknowledged, not yet monetised

Asked directly about data-centre power demand, management confirmed active team-building and exploration but was explicit that "the profitability metrics" are still being worked out on both cost and pricing, declining to commit to a segment entry until returns are clear. Treated here as a real optionality, not a near-term revenue driver.

Bottom line

What's good: a genuinely rare combination for an Indian IPP — DSO collapsed from 181 to 20 days as the book shifted toward central, cash-discounting offtakers; core EBITDA margins have held an 87–92% band through a period of very fast capacity build; the company appears to be the largest single BESS operator in the country by its own account, monetising early-mover merchant arbitrage at 80%+ margins while a genuinely sophisticated capex-deferral tactic protects returns on delayed sites; guidance on BESS commissioning has been raised twice, not cut, within this three-month window; and financing access (REC, PFC, NaBFID, AA-/Stable ratings) remains diversified and improving on cost. What's not good: FY26 operating cash flow fell 19% even as normalized PAT rose 85.6%, a real earnings-quality flag even if management's supplier-advance explanation is credible and corroborated by the DSO data; 71% of FY26 pre-tax profit was non-power-sale treasury/DSRA interest income; total debt more than doubled in FY26 before being partially offset by a 16.5%-dilutive QIP whose own debt-repayment proceeds are being deployed well behind schedule; free cash flow has been negative and worsening for three straight years with no path to self-funding disclosed; two senior executives (CFO, SVP R&D) departed within seven weeks of each other; the joint statutory auditor pairing changed year-on-year without explanation in this dataset; and a BESS facility fire in July, while financially immaterial as disclosed, is a live reminder of physical risk in a fleet that is roughly tripling in size this year. How management has navigated it: candidly and specifically on the earnings calls reviewed — granular, numbers-backed answers to hard questions on curtailment, cash-flow divergence, BESS margin sustainability and capex deferral, plus a fast, detailed, quantified disclosure of the July fire incident rather than a vague statement. Guidance has consistently moved in the direction of "more/sooner," never "less/later," across the two-quarter window this report can observe.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood the growth and execution story keeps delivering against its own guidance over the next 12 months: moderately favourable, roughly 55–65%. The operating fundamentals — DSO, margin resilience, BESS scale, financing access — are stronger and more independently corroborated than the market's occasional "priced-for-perfection" framing might suggest at 41x trailing earnings. But the earnings-quality flag on cash flow, the still-heavy absolute debt load, the recurring dilution pattern, the management-acknowledged shelf life on merchant BESS spreads, and a valuation sitting near a 52-week high with a newly-appeared strong-sell rating in the mix all argue against treating this as a low-risk compounder. This reads as a name to own selectively through the execution window rather than to chase at the current multiple without a plan for what happens if a single guided milestone (a GNA delay, a BESS spread compression, a weaker annual CFO print) slips.
