Equity Research Note · Power Transmission, Distribution & Utility Infrastructure

Adani Energy Solutions Limited

NSE: ADANIENSOL · BSE: 539254 · FY2023–FY2027(Q1) performance review, BSE-filing, earnings-call and investor-presentation synthesis, as of 22 August 2026.

The one-line story: AESL is mid-way through a genuine step-change — the Mumbai HVDC line fully commissioned in March 2026, smart-meter installs blowing past the FY26 target (83 lakh vs. 70 lakh guided), and a brand-new Energy Solutions Platform contributing ₹590 crore of segment EBITDA in its very first full quarter — and Q1FY27 delivered on it: consolidated EBITDA up 58% YoY to a record ₹3,178 crore and PAT up 130% to ₹1,237 crore. But the growth is being bought with leverage that management itself is watching closely: net debt/EBITDA sits at 4.5x, at the low end of the company's own 4.5–4.7x ceiling, funding a capex programme guided at ₹21,000–25,000 crore a year through FY28; free cash flow has been negative in three of the last four fiscal years; payable days nearly doubled to 132 in FY26; and the CFO who set that FY27 guidance in April resigned "for personal reasons" five weeks later, with a new CFO delivering the much stronger Q1 print. A large chunk of Q1's Energy Solutions upside was explicitly flagged by management as monsoon-timing-driven rather than structural.

01

What AESL does, and how it has grown

India's largest private transmission company and a top-rated Mumbai/Mundra distribution utility, now adding two new growth legs — smart metering and an integrated energy-solutions/trading platform — on top of the regulated core.

Incorporated in 2013 and renamed from Adani Transmission Limited to Adani Energy Solutions Limited in July 2023, AESL is headquartered in Ahmedabad and operates through four reportable segments (per the FY26 consolidated segment note): Transmission (power transmission lines, largely Tariff-Based Competitive Bidding/TBCB fixed-tariff assets plus some cost-plus Regulated Asset Base/RAB assets), Distribution (retail power distribution in Mumbai through subsidiary Adani Electricity Mumbai Ltd/AEML, plus the Mundra SEZ distribution business), Smart Meter (smart-metering rollout under Design-Build-Finance-Own-Operate-Transfer/DBFOOT contracts with state discoms), and the newly renamed Energy Solutions Platform (formerly "Trading" — power procurement/supply structuring for C&I customers and data centres, plus commodity/power trading). A residual "Others" segment captures EPC contracting. AESL operates as a subsidiary of the S.B. Adani Family Trust (MarketData/overview.md); its Non-Executive Chairman is Gautam S. Adani (annual report, board profile).

A four-year build from a pure transmission utility to a diversified platform

Management's own framing on the Q4FY26 call is a useful anchor: standalone operating profit (their term, roughly core EBIT) was "range-bound between ₹4,000 crore and ₹4,500 crore" for four straight years, FY20–FY23, before breaking out to "closer to ₹6,000 crore" in FY24, "₹7,000 crore" in FY25, and "about ₹8,000 crore" (consolidated EBITDA ₹8,726 crore, confirmed in the same call) in FY26. That inflection continued directly into Q1FY27: consolidated EBITDA of ₹3,178 crore in a single quarter is already 36% of the entire FY26 total. The step-up is attributable to three concurrent developments: the Mumbai HVDC line (Kudus–Aarey, ~₹7,000 crore project cost, commissioned in full on 15 March 2026 — the world's first compact HVDC substation, per News, 15 Apr 2026), the smart-meter rollout scaling past its own FY26 target, and the Energy Solutions Platform going from an "incubation" business management first flagged verbally on prior calls to a full ₹1,906 crore-revenue, ₹590 crore-EBITDA segment in Q1FY27 alone.

The moat, as evidenced in the filings

AESL's most defensible asset is regulatory: TBCB-won transmission and DBFOOT-won smart-meter contracts carry long-dated, largely availability- or installation-linked tariffs (20–25-year concessions), and RAB-based assets (AEML distribution, the new Mumbai HVDC line) earn a regulator-set return on an expanding asset base rather than competing on price quarter to quarter. Management is explicit that this RAB growth is the core engine: AEML's Regulated Asset Base has grown from ₹5,400 crore at acquisition (2018) to over ₹10,500 crore today (Q4FY26 call), and the transmission RAB has added roughly ₹7,000 crore via the single Mumbai HVDC project. A second, harder-to-replicate asset is credit quality under scale: management highlighted that most individual project SPVs now carry AAA or AA+ domestic ratings even as consolidated capex has roughly tripled since FY23 (annual capex FY23 ₹4,702 crore → FY26 ₹14,432 crore, §05) — unusual discipline for a fast-scaling infra platform, and the credit-rating filings reviewed (CRISIL/ICRA reaffirmations through June–August 2026, §07) corroborate that the parent AESL/AEML ratings have not been downgraded through this expansion. What is not independently verifiable from this filing set is the "India's #1 distribution company" and "largest smart-metering platform" claims — both are management's own framing rather than a third-party ranking found in the reviewed documents.

⚠
EBITDA margin is compressing on a percentage basis — largely a mix effect, not a demand or pricing problemNormalized EBITDA margin has fallen every year shown, from 46.2% (FY23) to 30.6% (FY26). The driver is revenue mix, not deteriorating unit economics in the core regulated businesses: EPC/construction revenue under Ind AS 115 Service Concession Arrangements (recognised at cost-plus-nil-margin during the build phase), the Energy Solutions Platform's inherently lower-margin power-trading/pass-through revenue, and the fast-scaling smart-meter construction line all carry structurally thinner margins than transmission's core availability-based tariff revenue, and all three are growing faster than the core. Segment-level margins in §03 show transmission and distribution both still expanding EBITDA at 42%/18% YoY in Q1FY27 — the consolidated percentage compression is a denominator effect from mixing in lower-margin lines, which investors should not read as core-business margin erosion without checking the segment table.

What drove FY26 and Q1FY27

FY26 PAT growth of 115.3% is real and broad-based rather than a low base effect: FY25's own PAT was already the trough year for the reasons in the caption above, and FY26 EBITDA itself grew a genuine 15.4% while finance costs and D&A rose more slowly in relative terms (interest coverage improved to 1.91x from 1.34x), which is the opposite of what a one-off-driven recovery would look like. Q1FY27's 130% PAT growth is more segment-concentrated: ₹570 crore of the Energy Solutions Platform's ₹590 crore EBIT came from long-term power-supply positions rather than trading/services (per CEO Kandarp Patel on the Q1FY27 call), and management was explicit under analyst questioning that this quarter's strong merchant-market pricing was influenced by a delayed monsoon pushing power demand and prices higher than the prior year — a factor management itself flagged as not fully repeatable quarter to quarter ("there will also be quarter-to-quarter variability, because the average rate in the market during summer period... would be higher as compared to the monsoon or winter"). Investors should not extrapolate Q1FY27's Energy Solutions margin run-rate linearly into subsequent quarters without discounting for this.

Company's own leverage metric: 4.5x net debt/EBITDA in Q1FY27, at the low end of its 4.5–4.7x guided band

On the Q4FY26 call, CFO Kunjal Mehta reiterated a standing leverage policy: "we'll continue to maintain that leverage in the ratio of around 4.5x to 4.7x." The Q1FY27 investor presentation discloses the company's own net-debt/EBITDA figure at 4.5x, up from 3.2x at FY25 — inside the guided band, though at its very floor rather than comfortably within it, and using a narrower net-debt definition (excludes ₹1,405 crore of unsecured shareholder sub-debt) than the broader yfinance-derived 5.67x shown in the table above. Both measures point the same direction: leverage has risen meaningfully as capex accelerated, and management's own guided ceiling leaves limited further headroom before the next major capex wave (FY28 guidance of ₹22,000–25,000 crore, below) would need to be funded by more equity rather than debt alone — which is consistent with the July 2026 QIP actually happening.

Capital raised in this window: ₹3,500 crore QIP, plus a ₹3,050 crore acquisition

AESL closed a Qualified Institutional Placement on 30 July 2026, allotting 2,16,71,826 equity shares at ₹1,615 per share (a 3x-oversubscribed book, per News) for gross proceeds of ₹3,500 crore, earmarked for capex, debt repayment and acquisitions. The largest named use is the binding agreement (signed 9 June 2026) to acquire 100% of IntelliSmart Infrastructure Private Limited from NIIF and the Energy Efficiency Services Limited for ₹3,050 crore — still subject to Competition Commission of India approval as of the Q1FY27 call (22 Jul 2026) and therefore not yet consolidated into any of the financials in this report. Contingent liabilities & commitments: immaterial legacy claims, one legacy dispute resolved

Total contingent liabilities were ₹1,799.21 crore at FY26-end versus ₹1,795.05 crore at FY25-end (annual report, note 44) — essentially flat and equal to roughly 2% of consolidated net worth, dominated by a ₹1,381.28 crore legacy claim from Vidarbha Industries Power Limited relating to FY19 fuel costs (unchanged YoY, meaning no fresh escalation) and various tax/service-tax disputes, none individually material. Separately, the FY26 annual report discloses that Reliance Infrastructure Limited's 2022 arbitration claims under the original AEML share-purchase agreement were "amicably resolved" during the year, with R-Infra withdrawing all claims — a resolved legacy overhang rather than a new one.

Other call & disclosure insights

Management's own words on right-of-way, China-sourcing, the data-centre opportunity, and a leverage debate they visibly did not want to have on record.

Right-of-way: managed proactively, not eliminated

Asked twice across the two calls about right-of-way (ROW) challenges — a chronic industry issue in Indian transmission construction — management's answer was consistent: AESL does not rely solely on state-machinery compensation processes, runs its own parallel ground-level negotiation process, and concentrates projects in regions where it already has operating presence and local knowledge. CEO Kandarp Patel stated explicitly that "no project of AESL got delayed because of delay in the equipment delivery," a supply-chain claim distinct from (and not addressing) potential ROW-driven delays, which management characterised as present but manageable rather than resolved.

No exposure to Chinese equipment on TBCB projects

In response to a direct analyst question about Chinese-sourced equipment shortages, management stated AESL "can't source equipment from China" for its Tariff-Based Competitive Bidding projects (a bid-condition restriction) and does not envisage any such imports; separately noted that domestic supply-chain constraints for transformers, GIS and reactors are "easing out" as OEMs including GE and Hitachi expand India manufacturing capacity.

The leverage question management visibly deflected

A shareholder did the borrowing-level math live on the Q4FY26 call — and got a promise to "walk through it separately," not an answerLong-time shareholder Bharat Shah calculated that maintaining the stated 4.5–4.7x leverage ratio through a high-growth profit phase implies absolute borrowings reaching ₹1,25,000–1,50,000 crore. Data-centre demand: real, but described as still nascent

On the Q1FY27 call, management (Raj Kumar Jain, Head of Energy Solutions Platform) quantified data-centre-specific Energy Solutions volume as "very negligible" currently (one contract, ~20–25 MW), against a much larger disclosed market-potential figure of 7.5+ GW by 2030–31 across data centres, distribution-company round-the-clock contracts and conventional C&I. The company has secured ~4 GW of renewable supply (~4,000 MW from Adani Green Energy, the balance third-party) against this opportunity but has tied up demand-side contracts for only ~350 MW of that capacity so far, with the balance sold on exchanges/bilateral contracts in the interim — underscoring that the platform's current financial contribution is running ahead of its long-term-contracted, annuity-like end state that management says it is working toward.

Bottom line

What's good: a genuine, multi-year earnings inflection that is now showing up in hard numbers, not just narrative — FY26 PAT +115.3%, Q1FY27 EBITDA +58% and PAT +130% YoY, all cross-checked across the financial-results filing, investor presentation and MarketData; the Mumbai HVDC line commissioned on time and management already guiding its specific FY27 tariff contribution; a smart-meter rollout that beat its own FY26 target (83 lakh vs. 70 lakh guided) and is tracking toward the FY27 target after one quarter; consistently strong and growing operating cash flow that has run 3.0x–8.5x reported PAT every year since FY23, a clean earnings-quality signal; capex running 2.9x–7.3x depreciation, unambiguous evidence of real capacity expansion; capital commitments that tripled in FY26, suggesting the forward capex guidance is substantially contracted, not aspirational; a clean, unmodified audit opinion; stable-to-improving domestic and international credit ratings even as the balance sheet has scaled sharply; a legacy R-Infra arbitration resolved amicably during the year; and a leverage ratio that, on management's own metric, sat inside its stated 4.5–4.7x policy band as of Q1FY27.

What's not good: free cash flow has been negative in three of the last four fiscal years, including the largest shortfall on record in FY26 (−₹3,435 Cr), as capex growth (+53.9% YoY) outran even strong CFO growth (+21.6% YoY); payable days nearly doubled to 132 in FY26, a working-capital metric that management has not directly addressed on record; EBITDA margin has compressed on a percentage basis for four straight years as low-margin construction/trading revenue grows faster than the core; a material share of Q1FY27's headline growth reflects a low prior-year base in one new segment and a monsoon-timing tailwind management itself flagged as not fully repeatable; the CFO who set the FY27/FY28 guidance departed five weeks after the call that gave it; a specific, shareholder-calculated implication of the company's own leverage policy (₹1,25,000–1,50,000 Cr of eventual borrowings) was raised on the Q4FY26 call and deferred rather than answered; one News-sourced Q4FY26 PAT figure does not reconcile against the company's own filed numbers; a non-executive director remains under a US DOJ/SEC matter pending final court approval; and Q1FY27's actual capex run-rate, annualised, tracks below the low end of FY27 guidance after one quarter.

How management has navigated it: with real specificity on operational metrics (smart-meter counts, ckm added, segment capex splits down to the crore) and real discipline about not over-promising on EBITDA — the one number given (₹11,500 Cr FY27) was extracted under direct, repeated shareholder questioning rather than volunteered. That same discipline showed a limit on the borrowing-level question in §08, where a specific, uncomfortable calculation was deferred rather than engaged with. The CFO transition appears to have been absorbed cleanly — guidance was not reset, and the new CFO's first quarter beat rather than missed — but five weeks is a short runway to judge genuine continuity, and this report's next update should specifically check whether the FY27 capex and EBITDA guidance are being tracked to, or quietly walked back.

AI-Synthesized ViewLikelihood that AESL's own FY27 guidance (~₹11,500 Cr EBITDA, ₹21,000–22,000 Cr capex, 4.5–4.7x leverage) is substantially met over the next 12 months: moderate-to-high, roughly 55–65%. The quantitative record supports the growth story more than it undermines it — cash conversion is clean, capex commitments are substantially contracted, and Q1FY27 delivered rather than disappointed — but the business is running with limited leverage headroom against an aggressive multi-year capex plan, a fresh CFO, a still-unanswered question about the endpoint of its own borrowing policy, and a valuation that already prices in a meaningful share of the guided step-up. This is a story where the operational execution has, so far, been better than the balance-sheet discipline required to fund it comfortably — and the next two to three quarters should show clearly which of those two trends is actually in charge.
